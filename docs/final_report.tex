\begin{abstract}
We trained twenty diffusion-model variants ($>$120h of GPU training) to generate synthetic limit
order book (LOB) data for Intel, Tesla, and the S\&P~500 ETF, varying
five architectural design choices (prediction target, conditioning,
preconditioning, data transform, loss weighting). Each variant was
evaluated on three independent quality axes: distributional realism
(do per-feature statistics match real data?), temporal transferability
(can a downstream model trained on synthetic data predict real
returns?), and multivariate coverage (does the generator span the full
range of observed market states?). No architecture wins all three.
The model with the most realistic marginal distributions is the least
useful for downstream training; the model most useful for training
covers none of the real state space. The trade-off is partly
mechanical --- per-element squared-error loss cannot simultaneously
optimize marginal shape and cross-feature temporal structure on
heavy-tailed data --- and partly stock-specific: it is sharp on INTC
(a large-tick stock with 89\% zero-return events) but largely
dissolves on TSLA (a small-tick stock with more continuous returns).
As a secondary application we stress-tested four Avellaneda--Stoikov
market-making agents against each generator and found that
agent-ranking metrics are decoupled from all three quality axes. For
practitioners the implication is that the right generator depends on
which quality axis the downstream task rewards.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{The Backtesting Problem}
\label{sec:backtest_problem}

Historical replay is non-responsive --- the recorded book does not
react to the agent under test, systematically over-crediting
impact-bearing strategies (see \cite{berti2025trades} for a recent
treatment). Synthetic generators are the standard fix. The open
question is which generator architecture produces LOB streams
realistic enough to be useful.

\subsection{Why Diffusion Models for LOBs}
\label{sec:why_diffusion}

GANs suffer mode collapse on heavy-tailed
data~\cite{li2020generating, coletta2021towards, coletta2022learning};
denoising diffusion probabilistic models~\cite{ho2020denoising}
replace the adversarial dynamic with an explicit denoising
objective, train stably, and admit flexible conditioning. The
TRADES work~\cite{berti2025trades} is the first applied diffusion
architecture for LOB simulation and is our methodological anchor:
we adopt their transformer denoiser, their event-tensor
representation, and their predictive-score evaluation protocol,
and ask what happens when the architectural design choices around
that backbone are varied systematically.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This report makes five contributions. The diffusion machinery itself
is not new, but what the machinery does on this kind of data, when
varied systematically, is.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A systematic ablation of \textbf{twenty diffusion-model
    checkpoints} across five orthogonal architectural axes (prediction
    target, conditioning, preconditioning, data transform, loss
    weighting), all sharing the TRADES backbone~\cite{berti2025trades}
    and trained on the same INTC corpus.
    \item Identification of a \textbf{three-axis tension} between
    distributional realism, temporal transferability, and multivariate
    state-space coverage --- no checkpoint we trained wins all three,
    and the per-axis rankings are systematically anti-correlated across
    architecture families.
    \item A mechanistic account of \emph{why} the tension arises:
    per-element MSE on heavy-tailed multivariate targets cannot
    simultaneously match per-feature marginals and joint coverage, and
    rank-preserving marginal fixes scramble the per-event values that
    the predictive-transferability evaluation rewards.
    \item Stress-testing of the four-agent Avellaneda-Stoikov family
    against each generator. Agent-ranking metrics
    decouple from the three primary quality axes, but the
    historical-replay reference is itself biased (non-responsive); we
    therefore report the empirical patterns that survive across the
    spread of generator-induced rankings rather than treating any
    single generator's ranking as ground truth.
    \item \textbf{Cross-asset validation} on INTC (large tick), TSLA
    (small tick), and SPY (index ETF, in flight at submission), which
    qualifies the structural-vs-microstructure-specific reading of the
    three-axis tension --- on TSLA the tension partially dissolves, so
    the picture from INTC alone overstates how universal the trade-off
    is.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limit Order Book Mechanics}
\label{sec:lob_mechanics}

We refer readers unfamiliar with LOB mechanics to
\cite{gould2013limit}. The structural property of INTC that
matters for this study is that it is a large-tick stock: the
spread is pinned at one tick ($\sim$3.3~bps) and roughly $89\%$ of
events leave the midprice unchanged. The per-event return is
therefore a heavily zero-inflated quantity rather than a
continuous Gaussian-like one, and this zero-inflation becomes
load-bearing in \S\ref{sec:copula_story}.

\subsection{Data Source and Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:data_source}

Raw data is NYSE TAQ via WRDS (nanosecond-resolution quote + trade
messages); we reconstruct the top-of-book LOB state stream with a
LOBSTER-style pipeline. The diffusion model operates on an
eight-feature event tensor capturing the inside quote and the most
recent trade: $\Delta t$ (time gap to previous event, log-scaled),
$\text{event\_type}$ (categorical, one-hot), $\text{bid\_dist}$
and $\text{ask\_dist}$ (best-level distance from midprice, ticks),
$\text{bid\_sz}$ and $\text{ask\_sz}$ (top-of-book queue sizes),
$\text{trade\_sz}$ and $\text{trade\_sign}$ (zero unless the event
is a trade), and $\text{mid\_return}$ (relative midprice change).
Numeric features are $z$-scored on training-window statistics. The
diffusion input/output unit is a $256$-event non-overlapping
window; ten windows are stitched at sampling time into a
$2{,}560$-event pseudo-day, the unit on which all evaluation in
\S\ref{sec:results} is computed. Two correlation properties recur
later: $\text{bid\_dist}$ and $\text{ask\_dist}$ are mechanically
anti-correlated, and the order-flow-imbalance signal A2 trades on
(Appendix~\ref{app:agents_impl}) predicts $\text{mid\_return}$ at
Spearman $\sim$0.07~\cite{cont2014price} --- real but faint, and
straightforward to scramble.

\subsection{Train / Validation / Holdout Split and Regime Labelling}
\label{sec:splits}

We use a temporal $60 / 10 / 20$ day split for INTC: the first sixty
trading days train the diffusion model, the next ten pick checkpoints
and tune hyperparameters, and the final twenty are the holdout window
that produces every number reported in \S\ref{sec:results},
\S\ref{sec:agents}, and \S\ref{sec:cross_asset}. The holdout is never
seen during training or model selection. Each day in the corpus is
additionally tagged with one of four regime labels --- \emph{base},
\emph{high\_vol} (top quartile of realized variance), \emph{toxic} (top
quartile of VPIN~\cite{easley2012flow}), and \emph{thin} (bottom
quartile of trade volume) --- and that label is the conditioning vector
consumed by the AdaLN-Zero modulation block of \S\ref{sec:conditioning}.
Regime conditioning is what lets the generator produce stress days on
demand rather than only the average day; without it a downstream user
who wants a high-volatility scenario would have to keep sampling and
filtering. The TSLA and SPY corpora used in
\S\ref{sec:cross_asset} follow the same split convention but with
ticker-specific regime thresholds (the absolute volatility level that
counts as ``high\_vol'' on SPY is much lower than on INTC).

\subsection{Stylized Facts of Financial Returns}
\label{sec:stylized_facts}

We evaluate against six Cont (2001) stylized
facts~\cite{cont2001stylized} --- heavy tails (kurtosis
$\approx 755$ on INTC), volatility clustering, absence of return
autocorrelation, trade-sign persistence (lag-1 ACF
$\approx 0.67$), volume-volatility correlation, and the leverage
effect --- operationalized into sixteen G1 pass/fail checks
(Appendix~\ref{app:g1_matrix}; Figure~\ref{fig:real_stylized_facts}
shows the empirical realization on a 10-day INTC val subset).

\subsection{Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models}
\label{sec:ddpm}

A denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM)~\cite{ho2020denoising}
defines a forward Markov chain that gradually corrupts a clean sample
$\mathbf{x}_0$ into Gaussian noise $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},
\mathbf{I})$ over $T$ steps with a fixed variance schedule
$\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$, and a learned reverse chain $p_\theta$ that
denoises $\mathbf{x}_T$ back to a sample from the data distribution.
With $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$,
the forward marginal at step $t$ is
$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon$
with $\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$, and the
training loss in the standard $\epsilon$-prediction
parameterization is
$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol\epsilon}
\bigl[\,\lVert\boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c})\rVert_2^2\,\bigr]$,
i.e.\ per-element MSE on the noise prediction conditioned on optional
side information $\mathbf{c}$ (in our case a regime label). We use
$T=1000$ steps with the cosine variance schedule of
\cite{nichol2021improved} and a transformer denoiser following
TRADES~\cite{berti2025trades}. All twenty checkpoints share this
backbone; they differ only along the five design axes of
\S\ref{sec:design_space}.

\subsection{The Architectural Design Space}
\label{sec:design_space}

We organize the design space around five axes that each control a
different piece of the training/sampling pipeline: the prediction target
(what the network regresses against), the conditioning mechanism (how the
regime label enters), the preconditioning (how loss magnitude is
normalized across noise levels), the data transform (what space we
diffuse in), and the loss weighting (which timesteps the gradient is
allowed to favor). The remainder of \S\ref{sec:design_space} introduces
each axis in order, and \S\ref{sec:checkpoints} reports which of the
$2{\times}2{\times}2{\times}3{\times}2 = 48$ possible cells we actually
trained.

\subsubsection{Prediction Target: $\eps$ vs $v$}
\label{sec:pred_target}

The standard $\eps$-prediction parameterization of \cite{ho2020denoising}
asks the network to regress the noise added at step $t$. Because the
loss is per-element MSE and is dominated by the high-noise regime where
$\boldsymbol\epsilon$ already overwhelms signal, the gradient is
mode-covering: it converges to a denoising function that averages over
plausible reconstructions and undershoots tail magnitudes. The
\emph{velocity} parameterization $v$ of \cite{salimans2022progressive}
re-targets the network onto
$\mathbf{v}_t \equiv \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0$,
which makes loss variance more uniform across noise levels and gives the
gradient at low noise more leverage to commit to the actual signal.
Empirically this sharpens marginal tails (\S\ref{sec:res_g1}) but, as
\S\ref{sec:res_pca} reports, the per-element nature of the criterion
means the model commits to extreme values without coordinating those
extremes across features. Both targets are end-to-end MSE
parameterizations; the difference is which quantity the per-element
gradient is asked to drive to zero.

\subsubsection{Conditioning: FiLM vs AdaLN-Zero}
\label{sec:conditioning}

The regime label and timestep embedding need to enter every transformer
block. FiLM~\cite{perez2018film} computes per-block scale and shift
parameters $(\gamma_\ell, \beta_\ell)$ from the conditioning vector and
applies $\gamma_\ell\odot\mathbf{h}_\ell + \beta_\ell$ to the block's
hidden state --- four modulation parameters per block. The AdaLN-Zero
mechanism of the diffusion transformer (DiT)~\cite{peebles2023scalable}
instead replaces the block's LayerNorm with an adaptive one whose
scale/shift \emph{and} a residual gate are produced by the conditioning
vector, with the gate initialized to zero so each block begins as the
identity --- six modulation parameters per block, plus a stable warm-up
trajectory. We tested both. FiLM-conditioned variants in our Phase F
runs exhibited a modulation-collapse failure mode in which $\gamma$
saturated near $1$ and $\beta$ near $0$, effectively disabling the
conditioning; AdaLN-Zero did not. All twenty checkpoints reported in
\S\ref{sec:results} use AdaLN-Zero.
Figure~\ref{fig:regime_tsne} shows the four regime embeddings each
of our three protagonists learned, projected to two dimensions by
t-SNE. The four regimes are spatially separated in all three panels
--- i.e.\ AdaLN-Zero is using the conditioning bandwidth to
differentiate \emph{base} / \emph{high\_vol} / \emph{toxic} /
\emph{thin} rather than collapsing them to a single embedding ---
which is the empirical control that the FiLM-collapse story above
implicitly relies on.

\subsubsection{Preconditioning: EDM (Karras et al.\ 2022)}
\label{sec:edm}

EDM~\cite{karras2022elucidating} reframes diffusion training around
a continuous noise scale $\sigma$ and wraps the denoiser with
$\sigma$-dependent input, output, skip, and noise scalings chosen so
the network's effective input and target magnitudes are order-unity
at every $\sigma$ --- the operating range stays comfortable
everywhere and the training loss landscape becomes more isotropic
across timesteps. The single free hyperparameter is
$\sigma_{\text{data}}$ (the assumed scale of the clean signal); we
swept $\sigma_{\text{data}} \in \{0.5, 1.0, 1.5\}$ across the v6 /
v7 / v7\_b checkpoints (Appendix~\ref{app:checkpoints}).

\subsubsection{Data Transform: z-score vs Copula}
\label{sec:copula_transform}

The default transform is per-feature $z$-scoring, which preserves
relative structure but leaves the diffusion model responsible for
learning the marginal shape. The copula construction
\cite{sklar1959fonctions} per-feature Gaussianizes the data via the
empirical CDF and probit
($z_i = \Phi^{-1}(\widehat{F}_i(x_i))$), leaving the diffusion
model to learn only the dependence structure; at sampling time the
inverse map $x_i = \widehat{F}_i^{-1}(\Phi(z_i))$ restores the
marginal shape, making per-feature G1 checks trivially correct by
construction. The complication is that for zero-inflated features
(e.g.\ INTC's $\sim 89\%$ zero-return events documented in
\S\ref{sec:stylized_facts}) $\widehat{F}_i$ has a step discontinuity
that breaks the diffusion target; dequantization
\cite{theis2016note} fixes it by smearing the point mass over a
small interval before the CDF is applied. v9 uses the
copula+dequantization pipeline; the failure mode of the naive
version (v8) is the case study of \S\ref{sec:copula_story}.

\subsubsection{Loss Weighting: Min-SNR-$\gamma$ (Hang et al.\ 2023)}
\label{sec:min_snr}

The Min-SNR-$\gamma$ scheme~\cite{hang2023efficient} caps the
per-step loss weight at
$\min(\mathrm{SNR}(t),\gamma)/\mathrm{SNR}(t)$ to prevent low-noise
(high-SNR) timesteps from dominating the gradient. We swept
$\gamma\in\{1, 5\}$; $\gamma{=}1$ over-corrects and all twenty
checkpoints in \S\ref{sec:results} use $\gamma{=}5$.

\subsection{The 20 Trained Checkpoints}
\label{sec:checkpoints}

A naive cross of the five axes gives forty-eight cells, more than we
could train under our compute budget. We instead trained twenty
checkpoints chosen along three logical arms. The \emph{scale-accurate
arm} (v1 -- v5, plus v6 / v7 / v7\_b) keeps the standard MSE-on-noise
recipe intact and varies prediction target, EDM preconditioning, and
$\sigma_{\text{data}}$. The \emph{scale-exploded arm} (v3\_e9\_noclip,
v3\_e25\_noclip, v3\_e10) drops gradient clipping and Min-SNR weighting
to expose how aggressive the network can become in the predictive
direction at the explicit cost of distributional realism --- this arm
is what populates the predictive-corner of \S\ref{sec:res_predictive}.
The \emph{copula arm} (v8, v8\_b, v9, v9\_b, v10, v11, v2\_remapped)
holds the backbone fixed and varies the data transform between
$z$-score, copula, and copula+dequantization, and sweeps
post-hoc remap variants of v2 to isolate which axis the copula moves
through. The full configuration matrix is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:checkpoints}; throughout \S\ref{sec:results} we lean
on three protagonists --- v2 (best G1), v5 (heaviest tails), v9
(highest PCA coverage) --- with the rest cited only when an ablation
makes the mechanism easier to read.

\subsection{Evaluation Framework: The Three Axes}
\label{sec:eval_framework}

A generator can fail a synthetic-LOB benchmark in qualitatively different
ways: it can match per-feature distributions but produce sequences whose
temporal structure breaks downstream models; it can produce sequences
that look temporally rich but only span a narrow region of the joint
state space; or it can do well on the per-tape average and badly on the
tail moments that actually matter for risk. We therefore evaluate each
checkpoint on three independent axes plus one consistency check.

\subsubsection{Distributional Realism (G1)}
\label{sec:eval_g1}

Sixteen stylized-fact checks following \cite{cont2001stylized}, plus a
small number of microstructure-specific additions (zero-return event
fraction, OFI distribution, trade fraction, conditional moments).
Each check is a pass / fail comparison of a synthetic statistic against
the corresponding real statistic, with tolerances calibrated against
the bootstrap interval of the same statistic computed on independent
real chunks. We report per-model pass count out of sixteen; the full
per-check matrix is given in Appendix~\ref{app:g1_matrix}.

\subsubsection{Temporal Pattern Transferability (Predictive Score)}
\label{sec:eval_predictive}

Following the TRADES protocol~\cite{berti2025trades}, we train a
two-layer LSTM on synthetic data to predict the next mid-price return
from the prior twenty events, evaluate it on a held-out real window,
and report the test MAE expressed as a multiple of the
train-on-real / test-on-real baseline. A score of $1.0$ means the
synthetic data is as useful as real data for training the LSTM;
$10.0$ means the LSTM trained on synthetic does ten times worse than
one trained on real. This is the standard ``train-synth, test-real''
methodology and answers the question: \emph{can a downstream sequence
model learn useful patterns from this synthetic data?}

\subsubsection{Multivariate Coverage (PCA Convex Hull)}
\label{sec:eval_pca}

Each 2{,}560-event tape is summarized by a fixed feature vector
(per-tape volatility, mean OFI, return skew and kurtosis, trade
fraction, and a small number of others); we fit a 2-D PCA on the real
tape feature vectors and project the synthetic tapes into the same
coordinates. Coverage is reported as the area of the intersection of
the synthetic convex hull with the real convex hull, divided by the
area of the real hull. A coverage of $100\%$ means synthetic spans the
joint state-space of real; $0\%$ means synthetic lives entirely outside
real's state-space.

\subsubsection{Agent-Based Ranking ($\rdiff$) and Why It Decouples}
\label{sec:eval_agents}

We additionally measure $\rdiff$, the Spearman rank correlation between
synthetic-data and historical-data terminal-PnL rankings of the
Avellaneda-Stoikov family of market makers we describe in
Appendix~\ref{app:agents_impl} (specifically agents A0--A3 from
\cite{avellaneda2008high, cont2014price, easley2012flow}). $\rdiff$ is
intended as a top-level practical check: if a generator preserves
agent rankings, it is at least usable for downstream strategy
comparison even when it fails one of the three primary axes. We flag in
advance the central finding of \S\ref{sec:agents}: $\rdiff$ does not
align cleanly with G1, predictive, or PCA, but the historical reference
ranking it is benchmarked against is itself biased (replay does not
respond to the agent's trades), so we treat $\rdiff$ as a consistency
check and report what survives the spread, not as a fourth
quality axis.

Table~\ref{tab:multi_axis} and Figure~\ref{fig:hero_three_axis}
show all twenty checkpoints on the three axes defined in

\subsection{Temporal Transferability Results}
\label{sec:res_predictive}

The headline ranking surprised us. The three protagonists land at
predictive $\approx 14\times$ (v2), $\approx 11\times$ (v5),
$\approx 106\times$ (v9) replay --- v2 and v5 within a factor of
two of each other, v9 an order of magnitude worse. Outside the
protagonists, the most informative reference points are the
\emph{noclip} family at the predictive corner (v3\_e9\_noclip
$4.0\times$, v2\_noclip $8.2\times$ --- the best two of our
twenty) and the copula+remapped family at the opposite corner
(v2\_remapped $266\times$, v8\_b $209\times$, v8 $117\times$,
v9\_b $\approx 100\times$). Full per-checkpoint numbers are in
Appendix~\ref{app:checkpoints}.

v9 (best marginals, $106\times$ replay) and v3\_e9\_noclip (worst
marginals, $4\times$) are the extremes: the model with $-0.43$
excess kurtosis and $43\times$ real's std produces the best
predictive score in the study, twenty-six times better than v9.
Scale matching is neither necessary nor sufficient for temporal
transferability.

The mechanism --- the LSTM rewards per-event value-sequence
fidelity, not marginal shape --- is unpacked in the v2\_remapped
control of \S\ref{sec:copula_lesson}.

\subsection{Multivariate Coverage Results}
\label{sec:res_pca}

The PCA-coverage axis asks a different question from G1 and predictive
score. We compute one feature vector per stitched 2,560-event tape (mean
and std of each of the eight canonical features), z-score using the real
training set's per-feature statistics, fit a 2-D PCA on the real tapes,
project the synthetic tapes through the same PCA, and report the area
of the intersection between the real convex hull and the synthetic
convex hull, divided by the area of the real hull. A perfect generator
matches real on this axis at $100\%$; a generator that produces tapes
too tight or too wide to overlap with real lands at $0\%$.

PCA coverage spans $0$--$85\%$ across our twenty checkpoints
(Appendix~\ref{app:pca_coverage_full}). The copula family
dominates ($85\%$) but with collapsed marginals (excess kurtosis
$\sim 2$ vs.\ real $755$); the protagonist trio sits at
$46$--$71\%$; the noclip family lands at $0\%$ despite hulls
$200$--$500\times$ real's area --- their projections are flung
entirely outside real's region. The pattern that wins on one axis
loses on another.

\subsection{Case Study: The Copula Story --- One Architecture, All Three Axes Move}
\label{sec:copula_story}
% Was its own §6 in earlier drafts; merged in here because it is the
% single most pedagogically rich instance of the §5 tension. Each of
% v8, v9, v2_remapped moves all three axes (G1, Pred, PCA) at once,
% and reading them in sequence makes the structural mechanism obvious.
% Approx 1-1.5 pages of the §5 budget.
%
% Also folds in the zero-inflation pathology that earlier drafts tried
% to introduce in §2: the empirical 89% point mass at mid_return = 0
% is the technical root cause of the copula failure, and earns its
% airtime here where it actually does load-bearing work.

Three of our twenty checkpoints belong to the same architectural
family: v8 (copula on the marginals, no dequantization), v9 (copula
\emph{with} dequantization), and v2\_remapped (no copula training,
post-hoc Spearman remap of v2's outputs to match real's marginals).
Each of the three was an attempt to solve the same problem --- the
zero-inflation in INTC mid-returns --- and each produces a different
trade-off across G1, predictive, and PCA.

v8's copula collapsed because INTC's $\sim 89\%$ zero-return mass
creates a CDF discontinuity that pins the diffusion model to a
degenerate $z$-band; Appendix~\ref{app:copula_mechanism} details the
four-step mechanism and shows the v8 / v8\_b / v9 z-space histograms.
The headline metrics: v8 G1 $= 8/15$ with excess kurtosis $2.6$ vs.\
real's predicted $400$--$800$, and v8\_b collapses to zero-variance
output --- \S\ref{sec:copula_fix} below shows how dequantization
recovered most of what v8 lost on G1, and \S\ref{sec:copula_lesson}
pins down why predictive transferability did not follow.

\subsubsection{The Fix (v9): Dequantization}
\label{sec:copula_fix}

The fix v9 implements is dequantization, a technique developed for
exactly this kind of CDF-discontinuity problem in the
normalizing-flow literature \cite{theis2016note}. Before fitting
$\hat{F}$, we detect each per-feature point mass with mass above a
threshold (here, anything $\ge 1\%$ of the training set), and we
spread its mass uniformly across a small interval $[v - \epsilon, v]$
where $v$ is the point-mass value and $\epsilon$ is calibrated to
the gap between $v$ and the nearest neighbouring value. For
\texttt{mid\_return} this means the $88.85\%$ point mass at
$x = 0$ is replaced by uniform noise on $[-\delta, 0]$ with $\delta$
small enough not to leak into the conditional-on-nonzero
distribution. The empirical CDF $\hat{F}$ now has no vertical jumps,
$z = \Phi^{-1}(\hat{F}(x))$ is a smooth bijection on the training
support, and the diffusion model trained in $z$-space sees a roughly
Gaussian input distribution rather than a degenerate one. At
inference time we apply the same noise injection to the synthetic
$z$ samples before running the inverse transform, so the dequantized
zeros decode back to a realistic mix of small-return events.

The recovery on $z$-space marginals is large.
\texttt{mid\_return}'s $z$-mean moves from $-2.55$ (v8) to $+0.001$
(v9), the $z$-std from $0.30$ to $0.28$ (still narrower than $1.0$
but inside the regime the diffusion model can sample from with
realistic spread), the KS statistic from $0.99$ (essentially
disjoint from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$) to $0.50$. The headline
distributional metrics follow: G1 jumps from v8's $8/15$ to v9's
$12/15$ (recovering four checks --- exactly the matched-pair number
in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_walks}), excess kurtosis from $2.6$ to
$129$ (back into the $\sim 100$--$200$ range that most non-noclip
checkpoints occupy), and trade fraction from $0.009$ to $0.049$
(the closest match to real's $0.048$ in the entire study). PCA
coverage drops from $85.2\%$ to $71.4\%$, but this is the right
direction --- v8's wide hull was wide \emph{because} the marginals
collapsed and pushed the projection off-center; v9's narrower hull
sits more inside real's region.

The trade-fraction recovery is not just a single moment matching:
it indicates the per-event composition has structurally changed.
Figure~\ref{fig:event_transitions} reads off the event-type
transition matrix per protagonist (probabilities of
quote$\to$quote, quote$\to$trade, trade$\to$quote, trade$\to$trade
across consecutive events). Real INTC: $\sim 95\%$
quote$\to$quote, with the other three transitions splitting the
remaining $5\%$. v9's stack is visually indistinguishable from
real ($\sim 94\%$ quote$\to$quote). v2 and v5 emit transition
matrices that are dominated by trade-events: $\sim 40\%$
trade$\to$trade and $\sim 40\%$ trade$\to$quote, with only
$\sim 20\%$ quote$\to$quote. v9 is the only protagonist that
reproduces real's event-process structure --- a property that no
metric in the G1 suite explicitly tests for, and that follows
mechanically from getting the trade fraction right.

    \caption{\textbf{Event-type transition matrix per protagonist.}

The one axis that does \emph{not} recover is predictive
transferability. v8 had Pred $= 117\times$ replay; v9 has Pred
$= 106\times$ --- essentially the same. Dequantization fixed the
copula's marginal-collapse pathology without doing anything for the
predictive cost. \S\ref{sec:copula_lesson} pins down why.

\subsubsection{The Deeper Lesson: Spearman $\neq$ Economic Information}
\label{sec:copula_lesson}

To isolate where v9's predictive cost comes from, we ran a control
experiment that has nothing to do with copula training. We took
v2 --- the baseline that already has Pred $= 14.2\times$ replay
(the best of our three protagonists) --- and applied a post-hoc
quantile remapping to its already-trained synthetic outputs. The
mapping is the simplest version of what copula+dequant does at
inference time: for each feature, sort the synthetic values, sort
the real validation values, and replace each synthetic rank-$k$
value with the corresponding rank-$k$ real value. Per-feature
marginals match real \emph{exactly} after this step; per-event
Spearman rank correlations between any two features are
\emph{preserved exactly} as well, because remapping each marginal is
a monotone transform and Spearman correlations are invariant under
monotone marginal transforms. The resulting checkpoint is
v2\_remapped.

If the predictive-score cost were specific to copula training (the
diffusion model learning a degenerate $z$-space, optimization
artifacts, anything model-internal), v2\_remapped should match
v2's predictive score because no training was redone. It does the
opposite. v2\_remapped's Pred jumps from v2's $14.2\times$ replay
to $\mathbf{266 \times}$ replay --- worse than v9's $106\times$, the
worst predictive score in the entire study. PCA coverage collapses
from v2's $58.6\%$ to $16.1\%$. G1 falls from $12/15$ to $9/15$.
Trade fraction \emph{does} fix from v2's $0.558$ to v2\_remapped's
$0.045$ (essentially exact match to real's $0.048$, the same
recovery v9 achieves). Marginal-distribution std comes up from
v2's $6 \times 10^{-6}$ to v2\_remapped's $10^{-4}$, much closer to
real's $2.2 \times 10^{-5}$.

The lesson the control imposes: the predictive cost we attribute to
``the copula'' is not specific to v9's diffusion training. \emph{Any}
operation that resamples per-event values to match a target marginal
distribution will pay the same cost, even when applied post-hoc to
an already-good checkpoint. The reason is the LSTM evaluation:
predictive transferability rewards getting the per-event return
\emph{value} sequence right (i.e.\ at every event index, the model
should produce a value close to what the real sequence had at
the same index given the same history); rank-preserving marginal
remapping shuffles those values --- ranks are preserved, but the
per-event values get re-bucketed onto a different marginal grid,
and the LSTM, which reads values not ranks, sees a sequence whose
predictability has been scrambled. The TRADES protocol
\cite{berti2025trades} measures something the copula-based
generators are intrinsically bad at, regardless of how clean the
copula transform is. \S\ref{sec:disc_structural} returns to the
practical implication: pick the generator whose evaluation axis
matches your downstream use case.

\paragraph{One last microstructure dynamic where v9 wins.}
The §5.7 case study so far has been negative on v9 for predictive
transferability and positive on v9 for marginal recovery. There is
one further test where v9 wins decisively: book resilience around
trade events. In a real market, when a buy trade hits the ask, the
depleted side (\texttt{ask\_sz}) drops sharply at the trade
instant, then gradually replenishes as new limit orders arrive ---
the canonical LOB resilience pattern of the microstructure
literature \cite{bouchaud2002statistical}. Figure~\ref{fig:book_resilience}
runs the event study: for each of $\sim 5000$ trades per source,
extract the depleted-side queue across $[t-30, t+30]$ events,
normalise to the pre-trade-window median, and report the median
plus IQR across trades. Real shows the canonical drop from $1.0$ to
$\sim 0.88$ at $t=0$ followed by partial recovery (the recovery
timescale on INTC is longer than $30$ events, so the curve only
returns part of the way). v9 tracks real almost identically (drop
to $\sim 0.90$, plateau around $0.90$). v2 and v5 stay flat at
$\approx 1.0$ across the entire window: their trade-saturated
event environment ($55$--$70\%$ trades, per \S\ref{sec:res_g1})
contaminates the per-trade dip with neighbour-trade activity that
averages the signal away. v9 reproduces a structural microstructure
dynamic that no marginal-distribution or per-feature correlation
metric in this report measures, and that v2/v5 fail at not because
their queue process is uniformly wrong but because their
\emph{trade-event arrival process} is.

\section{Agent-Based Stress Testing}
\label{sec:agents}

The point of building synthetic LOB generators is that we want to use
them. The use case we built into our pipeline from the start is
\emph{counterfactual market-making} --- deploying a market-making
agent against synthetic market scenarios that the agent has not seen
during calibration, so we can ask ``how would this agent behave in a
market that looks like INTC but isn't?''. This section reports what
happened when we ran four reference market-making agents through each
of our generators, what the rankings tell us, and where the
methodology runs out of road.

We deployed four Avellaneda-Stoikov agents (A0 constant-spread, A1
inventory-skewed baseline, A2 with order-flow-imbalance, A3 with
VPIN-gated widening; full specification in
Appendix~\ref{app:agents_impl}) against each generator in a
non-impact fill simulator. Because historical replay is
non-responsive (\S\ref{sec:backtest_problem}), recorded markets do
not react to the agent under test, and every PnL number we report
--- on every generator, including the historical-replay baseline ---
inherits the same blind spot. We therefore treat agent rankings as
a consistency check across heterogeneous synthetic environments, not
as a pseudo-truth: the answerable question is which patterns survive
the spread.

\subsection{Some Stylized Facts We Can Trust}
\label{sec:agent_implications}

Even without a ground-truth ranking, the spread of orderings in
Table~\ref{tab:rho_diff_spread} contains a few robust empirical
facts. The interpretive frame for the agent results sits alongside
one structural microstructure observation already established in
\S\ref{sec:copula_story}: of our three protagonists only v9
reproduces the LOB resilience signature around trade events
(Figure~\ref{fig:book_resilience}). Market-making strategies
depend mechanically on this resilience --- a posted limit order
gets ``protected'' by new arrivals after a trade depletes the
queue --- so any agent ranking on v2 or v5 is a ranking on a
microstructure environment that doesn't reflect what real INTC
gives a market maker to work with. We treat the agent-ranking
spread as informative about \emph{robustness} of each agent's edge
across heterogeneous synthetic environments, not as a pseudo-truth.

\paragraph{A0 wins (or near-wins) almost everywhere.} A0
(constant spread) is the best-PnL agent on truth and on four of
the seven generators; the only generators where A0 loses are v9
and v9\_b, which scrambled the per-event return-magnitude
structure A0 relied on for fee collection. The robust takeaway:
in non-impact backtests, a non-adapting MM that collects the
spread when crossed beats the inventory-adjusted AS variants ---
the well-known low-toxicity pattern \cite{avellaneda2008high}
that our generators reproduce.

\paragraph{The A1 vs.\ A2 distinction is a coin-flip.} A1 (no
signal) and A2 (OFI signal) sit adjacent in every generator's
ranking but the order between them flips across generators (A1
above A2 in v8\_b / v11; A2 above A1 in v8 / v10 / v2\_remapped),
and on truth A1 edges A2 by only $-0.08$ in PnL $z$-score. The
OFI signal A2 uses is real but weak; small per-event perturbations
flip which side wins. Takeaway: do not pick between OFI-aware and
OFI-blind market makers from a single backtest, synthetic or
historical.

\paragraph{A3 (VPIN-gated) does not consistently help.} A3
underperforms A1 in three of seven generators and A2 in five; on
truth A3 outperforms both, but inside a wide PnL spread. The
VPIN-as-toxicity-detector approach is fragile to the specific way
each generator emits trade size and sign, and our generators do
not agree on the direction of the fragility.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ~1.5 pages.

\subsection{Structural Result and Practical Implications}
\label{sec:disc_structural}

The empirical pattern that organizes \S\ref{sec:results} --- no
checkpoint wins all three axes, and the rankings are systematically
anti-correlated across pairs
(Table~\ref{tab:correlation_subset}) --- follows from the loss we
minimized. DDPM training is per-element MSE on a denoising target;
on heavy-tailed multivariate data this creates two pressures.
First, MSE is dominated by squared errors at large magnitudes, so
the $\eps$-objective settles into a mode-covering solution (v2:
kurtosis $268$ vs.\ real $755$, $30\%$ PCA); re-weighting toward
low noise ($v$-prediction) sharpens tails (v5 kurtosis $1257$) but
the per-element criterion commits to extremes without coordinating
them across features (v5 PCA $0\%$). Second, marginals can be hit
post-hoc by rank-preserving remap (the copula construction of
\S\ref{sec:copula_story}, extending
Sklar~\cite{sklar1959fonctions}), but re-bucketing per-event
values onto a target grid scrambles the sequence the LSTM reads as
``predictability'' --- which is why v9 and v2\_remapped both pay
the predictive cost of \S\ref{sec:copula_lesson}. The two
pressures share one root: per-element loss against multivariate
heavy-tailed targets. The TSLA result of
\S\ref{sec:cross_findings} qualifies how universal that root is.
On a smoother-tailed ticker the $v$-vs-$\eps$ trade-off does not
produce a Pareto front (v2 dominates v5 outright); the mechanism is
still real (TSLA v5 PCA $18.9\%$), but the \emph{trade-off cost}
depends on how heavy the marginals are. The three-axis tension is
structural \emph{conditional} on INTC-like microstructure, not
structural to MSE-based diffusion training in the abstract.

Practical implication: there is no one-size-fits-all generator,
but the choice is disciplined by which axis the downstream task
rewards. Table~\ref{tab:disc_use_cases} extracts the per-use-case
picks.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Six caveats bound the structural reading of
\S\ref{sec:disc_structural}; four directions follow naturally.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Backbone is fixed.} All twenty checkpoints share the
    TRADES transformer~\cite{berti2025trades}; the per-element-MSE
    mechanism has only been tested inside one architectural family.
    \item \emph{INTC-dominance.} The zero-inflated marginal that
    drives the \S\ref{sec:copula_story} pathology is INTC-specific;
    cross-asset evidence base is two-and-a-half tickers, not three.
    \item \emph{Thin holdout for agents.} Twenty days gives wide
    $\rdiff$ confidence intervals (\S\ref{sec:rdiff_decoupled});
    single-cell readings over-reach.
    \item \emph{Predictive score is LSTM-conditional.} A different
    downstream sequence model could re-rank the checkpoints.
    \item \emph{Backtests are non-responsive.} Every agent number
    assumes trades do not move the market; real impact would
    compress spreads and could reorder Table~\ref{tab:rho_diff_spread}.
    \item \emph{No intraday structure in synth output} (caveat
    visualized in Appendix~\ref{app:deep_diagnostic},
    Figure~\ref{fig:intraday_lob_volume}); downstream tasks needing
    time-of-day microstructure cannot use these tapes directly.
    \item \emph{Autoregressive diffusion.} Each event's denoising
    conditions on the realized previous event rather than the noised
    joint --- may ease the per-element MSE pressure that
    under-coordinates extremes.
    \item \emph{Mixture-of-experts denoising head.}
    $\eps$-prediction for bulk events, $v$-prediction for tail
    events, with a learned router --- one model carries both modes.
    \item \emph{Direct distributional losses} (MMD, Wasserstein)
    added to the MSE objective would reward shape directly rather
    than as a squared-error side effect.
    \item \emph{Broader evaluation.} More agents (momentum,
    market-taking, mean-reversion in addition to the MM family of
    Appendix~\ref{app:agents_impl}), longer holdouts, an
    impact-aware backtester; the SPY chained-training job
    (Appendix~\ref{app:reproducer}) will close the cross-asset
    triangle.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Sigma-vs-predictive non-monotonicity.}
The body §\ref{sec:res_predictive} reports the headline finding
in compressed form: scale matching is neither necessary nor
sufficient for predictive transferability. The
sigma-vs-predictive scatter (Figure~\ref{fig:pred_vs_scale_app})
plots the non-monotone relationship visually; the prose here
interprets it cluster-by-cluster. The twenty checkpoints fall
into three populated regions of the
$\sigma_{\text{synth}} / \sigma_{\text{real}}$ axis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{The scale-matched cluster}
    ($\sigma_{\text{synth}} \approx \sigma_{\text{real}}$): all
    eight of v2, v3\_e9, v3\_e19, v3p5, v4, v5, v6, v7, v7\_b.
    These checkpoints match the real-data return scale within
    a factor of two and span an order of magnitude on predictive
    ($10$--$36\times$ replay). They are the canonical
    ``well-behaved generator'' regime --- decent G1, decent PCA,
    middling predictive.
    \item \emph{The scale-exploded noclip family}
    ($\sigma_{\text{synth}} \approx 40\,\sigma_{\text{real}}$):
    v2\_noclip, v3\_e9\_noclip, v3p5\_noclip, v4\_noclip. These
    sit at the \emph{best} end of the predictive axis
    ($4$--$14\times$ replay) despite emitting returns
    $\sim 40\times$ wider than real. The mechanism: the LSTM reads
    per-event sign-and-order structure, not absolute magnitude,
    and the noclip family preserves the sign structure cleanly even
    though it loses the magnitude calibration.
    \item \emph{The copula family}
    ($\sigma_{\text{synth}} \approx 0.6\,\sigma_{\text{real}}$):
    v8, v8\_b, v9, v9\_b, v10\_cascade, v11, v2\_remapped.
    These sit at the \emph{worst} end ($28$--$210\times$ replay)
    despite emitting returns at roughly the right scale. The
    copula round-trip is essentially correct on marginals and on
    Spearman ranks but introduces enough per-event noise to
    wash out the predictable structure
    (\S\ref{sec:copula_lesson} unpacks the v2\_remapped control
    that isolates this mechanism).
\end{itemize}

The non-monotonicity is the load-bearing piece of evidence
behind \S\ref{sec:disc_structural}'s mechanism argument:
predictive transferability is decoupled from both marginal
realism and from scale calibration. Generators can fail the
predictive axis from either direction (too-narrow copula,
too-wide noclip) for different reasons, and they can pass it
while failing G1 (the noclip family does both).

\paragraph{Reading the two views together.}
The body \S\ref{sec:res_anticorrelation} Spearman matrix
(Table~\ref{tab:correlation_subset}) and the
Figure~\ref{fig:pairwise_anticorrelation_app} pairwise scatter
encode the same population-level information from different
angles --- the matrix collapses the data to rank correlations,
the scatter preserves the magnitudes. The
sigma-vs-predictive view of Figure~\ref{fig:pred_vs_scale_app}
adds a third dimension --- output magnitude --- that the
pairwise view collapses, and it is what makes the three-cluster
structure visible. Read together, the three exhibits localize
the three-axis tension: the protagonists v2/v5/v9 sit at
distinct cells of all three views, the noclip family lives at
the predictive-corner of Figure~\ref{fig:pred_vs_scale_app}
with $0\%$ PCA coverage, and the copula family inverts that
trade-off.

\paragraph{Coverage-dominant families.}
The two highest-coverage generators are v8 ($85.2\%$,
$2.6\times$ real's hull area) and v10\_cascade ($84.9\%$,
$2.0\times$). On the marginal-realism axis they are precisely the
worst performers in the study: v8's excess kurtosis is $2.6$
versus real's $755$, and v10\_cascade's is $4124$, both an order
of magnitude away from the protagonist trio. The mechanism is the
copula round-trip pushing the projection off-centre while
collapsing the marginal shape onto a near-Gaussian inside the
hull. Coverage and per-feature distributional realism are not the
same property --- a generator can paint a wide convex hull around
the right region while filling that hull with the wrong-shape
data.

\paragraph{The protagonist trio in context.}
The v2 / v5 / v9 protagonists sit in the
\emph{coverage-realistic} band ($46$--$71\%$, hull areas
$0.5$--$1.2\times$ real's $25.2$~PC1--PC2-area units), and the
trio's coverage ordering inverts the heavy-tail ordering: v9
($71.4\%$) covers the most despite having the most-restrictive
marginal recipe, v2 ($58.6\%$) sits in the middle, and v5
($46.1\%$) covers the least despite having the heaviest tails.
This is the cleanest small-sample evidence of the
predictive-vs-PCA $+0.57$ correlation
(Table~\ref{tab:correlation_subset}): family membership organizes
the trade-off, not any single axis on its own.

\paragraph{Noclip-family zero coverage.}
The three noclip checkpoints (v2\_noclip, v3\_e9\_noclip,
v3p5\_noclip) produce the largest synthetic hulls in the study
--- v3p5\_noclip's hull spans $486\times$ real's
PC1--PC2 area --- yet all three register $0\%$ coverage. The
mechanism is that removing the $x_0$ clip lets the model emit
extreme values that fall entirely outside real's PC1--PC2 region;
the spatial overlap with real's hull is empty regardless of how
much area the synth hull encloses. v4\_noclip is the partial
exception: it stays at $63.6\%$ coverage with a hull area
$1.1\times$ real's, suggesting the $v4 \to v4\_noclip$ walk
removes the clip without committing to the extremes the way the
other $\eps$-prediction noclip variants do.

\paragraph{Degenerate cases.}
v8\_b and v11 do not appear in the coverage table because PCA is
undefined when one or more feature has exactly-zero synthetic
standard deviation. v8\_b's degeneracy is the canonical
copula-collapse exemplar (Appendix~\ref{app:copula_mechanism},
Figure~\ref{fig:z_space_collapse}, centre panel); v11 emits
\texttt{mid\_return} $\approx 0$ everywhere, a separate failure
mode but with the same downstream consequence of an undefined
PCA. We keep these checkpoints in
Table~\ref{tab:checkpoint_metrics} and in the agent-ranking
analysis (\S\ref{sec:rdiff_decoupled}) because they remain
informative for those evaluation axes even when PCA is not
applicable.

\paragraph{v2 $\to$ v5 (full walk).}
v5 differs from v2 on two toggles: prediction target
($\eps \to v$) and conditioning (FiLM $\to$ AdaLN-Zero). Row $1$ of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation_walks} reports the matched-pair effect of
$\eps \to v$ alone: kurtosis drops by $\sim 30$ (the mode-covering
bias on bulk softens), predictive improves by $\sim 30\times$
replay, with essentially no effect on G1 ($\Delta = 0$). Row $2$
reports the effect of FiLM $\to$ AdaLN: predictive worsens by
$\sim 35\times$ replay, but kurtosis jumps by $+1184$ --- the
largest absolute kurtosis swing in the table. AdaLN-Zero supplies
enough conditioning bandwidth for the model to commit to extreme
samples, which is exactly what was needed to overshoot real's
kurtosis of $755$ to v5's observed $1257$. The net effect of the
two toggles together is the v5 we observe: better kurtosis,
marginally better predictive, slightly worse PCA, same G1. The
takeaway from this walk: \emph{tail behaviour is dominated by
conditioning bandwidth, not by prediction target}; the
$\eps \to v$ swap on its own does not produce heavy tails.

\paragraph{v2 $\to$ v9 (full walk).}
v9 differs from v2 on the data-transform axis only --- both are
$\eps$-prediction and FiLM-conditioned, but v9 wraps the raw
features through a copula transform with dequantization
(\S\ref{sec:copula_fix} unpacks the algorithmic detail;
Appendix~\ref{app:copula_impl} gives the per-feature treatment
table). The fourth and fifth rows of Table~\ref{tab:ablation_walks}
are the dominant deltas. $+$ copula alone drops G1 by $4$ checks,
pushes predictive up by $+103\times$ replay (severely worse), adds
$+27\%$ to PCA coverage, and \emph{collapses} trade fraction by
$-0.55$ (the largest single-toggle effect in the entire table ---
real's trade fraction is $\sim 0.048$, so a $-0.55$ swing closes
most of the v2-to-real gap on its own). $+$ dequantization then
recovers $+4$ G1 checks, gives back $-11\times$ on predictive, and
pushes kurtosis up by $+126$ back into the
$\sim 100$--$200$ range that most non-noclip checkpoints occupy.
The combination copula+dequant has a net effect on G1 of $0$,
$-92\times$ replay on predictive (still much worse than v2),
$+13\%$ on PCA, and $-0.51$ on trade fraction. v9 is what comes
out the other side: same G1 as v2, much worse predictive, better
PCA, dramatically better trade fraction. The takeaway: \emph{copula
buys distributional realism at the cost of predictive
transferability}, and dequantization mitigates but does not undo
that trade-off.

\paragraph{The noclip family (full walk).}
Removing the $x_0$ output clip --- the safety bound that keeps
each sampling step from emitting extreme values --- is not a path
that involves the protagonist trio (none of v2, v5, v9 use noclip),
but the toggle is too informative to skip. The last row of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation_walks} shows it loses $4.8$ G1 checks,
destroys PCA coverage ($-40.1\%$, often all the way to disjoint
hulls), and slightly worsens kurtosis ($-16$) --- in exchange for
the largest predictive-score improvement in the study
($-17\times$ replay). The mechanism is what
\S\ref{sec:res_predictive} flagged: removing the clip lets
the model emit return-sign sequences with realistic per-event
variation, which is exactly what the LSTM rewards. The cost ---
losing roughly four G1 stylized facts and all spatial overlap with
real --- shows that ``best predictive transferability'' and ``best
realism'' are not served by the same toggle. The three
protagonists demonstrate this trade-off pairwise across
architecture families; the noclip family demonstrates it
monotonically along a single toggle.

\paragraph{Ticker-selection rationale.}
The most common concern raised about §\ref{sec:results} is that
INTC is pathological. Across the $\sim 40$M events in our
validation set, $88.85\%$ of mid-return events are exactly zero
(\S\ref{sec:lob_mechanics}). If the three-axis tension is an
artifact of that one dataset feature, our conclusions should not
generalize to tickers with less zero-inflation. To test, we re-ran
the v2 and v5 training and evaluation pipelines on two qualitatively
different tickers chosen to bracket the INTC regime:
\textbf{TSLA} (small-tick single-name; smaller zero-return fraction,
more continuous returns) and \textbf{SPY} (the S\&P 500 ETF; deep
liquidity, tight spreads, index-arbitrage-driven price discovery).
Each combination is a fresh end-to-end pipeline: data prep, model
training, sampling, validation, TRADES metrics, and predictive
score on per-ticker train/val/holdout splits. We restricted the
cross-asset comparison to v2 and v5 because they are the two
architectural extremes from \S\ref{sec:three_protagonists} that
bracket the three-axis trade-off ($\eps$+FiLM, the predictive
winner; $v$+AdaLN, the heavy-tail winner). v9 (copula+dequant) was
deferred because the copula transform's per-feature dequantization
parameters are dataset-specific and would have required a second
calibration pass per ticker --- out of scope for the cross-asset
test.

\paragraph{Finding 1: TSLA v2 dominates v5 on every axis we measured.}
G1 ($12$ vs.\ $10$), predictive ($4.86$ vs.\ $12.2$), and PCA
($62.0\%$ vs.\ $18.9\%$) all favour v2. On INTC the same
architectural pair trades off --- v5 wins G1 and predictive while
v2 wins PCA --- so this is a genuine cross-ticker reversal of the
architectural ranking, not a re-confirmation. The clean ordering on
TSLA is the cleanest single piece of evidence we have that the
three-axis tension is partially INTC-specific: when the underlying
data is less zero-inflated, the heavy-tail-committing architecture
($v$-prediction + AdaLN-Zero) has no niche to dominate, and the
mode-covering baseline ($\eps$-prediction + FiLM) wins everywhere.

\paragraph{Finding 2: TSLA v2 produces the best non-broken predictive score in the study.}
At $4.86\times$ replay, TSLA v2 is the first non-distributionally-broken
generator we have to come within an order of magnitude of the
market-replay baseline. The previous best was the INTC noclip family
at $4.0\times$, which we documented as distributionally-broken in
\S\ref{sec:res_predictive} (G1 $= 6/15$, $0\%$ PCA hull). The
mechanical reading: TSLA's smaller tick and lower zero-fraction give
the LSTM more per-event signal to fit, and the $\eps$-prediction
baseline preserves enough of that signal to reach a regime where
downstream LSTM training transfers genuinely well.

\paragraph{Finding 3: TSLA v5's PCA coverage collapse confirms the $v$-prediction mechanism on smoother data.}
TSLA v5 lands at $18.9\%$ PCA coverage against INTC v5's
$46.1\%$. The same architectural mechanism that destroyed PCA
coverage on the INTC noclip family --- $v$-prediction commits the
model to extreme samples, and on data where the extremes don't fall
inside real's region the spatial overlap collapses --- operates on
TSLA v5 as well, just at a smaller magnitude. So the architecture-level
cost of $v$-prediction on a smoother-marginal dataset is real:
TSLA doesn't reward heavy-tail commitment, and v5 pays for it on
PCA coverage rather than on G1.

\paragraph{Trade-fraction note.}
TSLA's lower zero-fraction means the per-event composition of the
synthetic output is structurally closer to real even before any
generator-specific fix. The trade-fraction problem that motivated
the §\ref{sec:copula_story} copula case study on INTC is much less
acute on TSLA --- v2's TSLA trade fraction of $0.183$ is roughly
$4\times$ real's $\sim 0.05$, against INTC v2's $\sim 12\times$.
This is consistent with the §\ref{sec:disc_structural} reading
that the three-axis tension is structural \emph{conditional} on
INTC-like microstructure rather than to MSE-based diffusion training
in the abstract.

\subsection{Copula Transform Implementation}
\label{app:copula_impl}

The body §\ref{sec:copula_transform} introduces the copula-with-dequantization
transform at the design-axis level; the body §\ref{sec:copula_fix}
describes the dequantization step at narrative level. This appendix
provides the implementation companion: pseudocode for the
dequantization preprocessor, the per-feature treatment table that
records which features get dequantized at what threshold, and a
brief note on the inference-time inverse map.

\paragraph{Dequantization algorithm.}
The transform fits a per-feature empirical CDF $\widehat{F}_i$ on
the training set, applies $z_i = \Phi^{-1}(\widehat{F}_i(x_i))$
to every event, trains the diffusion model on the $z$-space, and
inverts the transform $x_i = \widehat{F}_i^{-1}(\Phi(z_i))$ on
synthetic outputs. Without dequantization, features with a
dominant point mass (most notably \texttt{mid\_return}'s
$88.85\%$ at zero) produce a step discontinuity in $\widehat{F}_i$
that the diffusion model collapses around
(Appendix~\ref{app:copula_mechanism} walks the four-step argument).
The dequantization preprocessor injects small uniform noise to
remove the discontinuity before the CDF is fit:

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Per-feature dequantization preprocessor (v9 pipeline)}
\label{alg:dequant}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Per-feature training values $\{x_i^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}$
\Require Point-mass detection threshold $\tau$ (default $0.01$)
\For{each feature $i$}
    \State Identify candidate point masses: values
    $v$ with empirical mass $\widehat{p}(v) \ge \tau$
    \For{each detected point mass $v$}
        \State Let $\delta_v$ = distance to nearest neighbouring
        training value below $v$ (or above, if $v$ is the
        smallest)
        \State Replace each training event with $x = v$ by
        $x \leftarrow v - U[0, \delta_v]$ (smear mass uniformly
        over $[v - \delta_v, v]$)
    \EndFor
    \State Fit empirical CDF $\widehat{F}_i$ on the smeared
    training values
    \State Compute $z_i = \Phi^{-1}(\widehat{F}_i(x_i))$ for every
    event (training and validation)
\EndFor
\State Train the diffusion model on the per-feature $z$-space
representation
\State \textbf{At inference time:} sample synthetic $z_i$ from the
diffusion model, apply the same noise injection
$z_i \leftarrow z_i + \eta$ with $\eta$ matched to the training-time
$U[0, \delta_v]$ scale on the back-transformed value, and decode
$x_i = \widehat{F}_i^{-1}(\Phi(z_i))$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Per-feature treatment.}
Of the eight features in the event tensor (\S\ref{sec:data_source}),
three have a dominant point mass on INTC and receive dequantization;
the other five are roughly continuous and are passed through
unchanged. Table~\ref{tab:dequant_treatment} records the
per-feature treatment.

\paragraph{$\delta$ calibration.}
The smearing interval width $\delta_v$ is chosen as the distance
from the point mass to the nearest neighbouring training value
\emph{below} it (or above, in the degenerate case of the point
mass being the smallest training value). For \texttt{mid\_return}
this gives $\delta \approx 10^{-7}$, smaller than the smallest
nonzero return in the training set and therefore comfortably
inside the conditional-on-nonzero distribution support without
leaking into it. The conservative choice prevents the dequantized
zeros from decoding back to non-trivial returns at inference time
while still smoothing the empirical-CDF discontinuity enough to
keep the diffusion target well-defined.

\paragraph{Inference-time symmetry.}
The inference-time noise injection (line $10$ of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:dequant}) is what closes the round-trip. Without
it, synthetic $z$ samples that land at the dominant-bucket value
would all decode back to exactly $x = v$ at inference, recreating
the point mass we worked to dissolve. With it, the synthetic
$z$ values that land near the bucket centre decode to a uniform
smear over the same $[v - \delta_v, v]$ interval, matching the
training-time treatment and producing a realistic mix of
small-return events around each previously-quantized value. v9's
trade-fraction match to real INTC ($0.049$ vs.\ $0.048$) is the
load-bearing evidence that this symmetry is implemented correctly.

\subsection{Copula Failure Mechanism}
\label{app:copula_mechanism}

This appendix expands the bridge paragraph at the start of
\S\ref{sec:copula_story}. Three pieces of context: (i) the
zero-inflation pathology that explains why a naive copula was
always going to fail on INTC; (ii) the design intent of the copula
approach that motivated v8 in the first place; (iii) the actual
v8 / v8\_b z-space collapse evidence with table and figure.

\paragraph{The zero-inflation pathology.}
INTC's mid-return distribution is not a smooth heavy-tailed
density. Across the $\sim 40$M events in our validation set,
$88.85\%$ of mid-return events are \emph{exactly} zero --- the
midprice did not change between consecutive events. Conditional on
a nonzero update, the distribution \emph{is} heavy-tailed:
conditional standard deviation $\sigma_{\neq 0} \approx
4.7\times 10^{-5}$, conditional excess kurtosis on the order of
$10^4$, conditional support extending out to $\pm 0.030$ (i.e.\
moves of $\sim 1{,}400\sigma_{\neq 0}$). The \emph{unconditional}
excess kurtosis we report against in the G1 suite (real $755$,
the headline reference) is what you measure when the $88.85\%$
point mass and the $11.15\%$ heavy-tailed continuous component are
mixed in a single distribution.

This mixed structure --- a discrete point mass at zero plus a
continuous heavy-tailed component --- is the property the copula
attempt is trying to fix. To see why the naive version cannot
work, trace the data flow:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The copula transform fits an empirical CDF $\hat{F}$ on
    the raw mid-return values, applies $z = \Phi^{-1}(\hat{F}(x))$
    to every event, trains the diffusion model on the $z$-space,
    samples in $z$-space, and inverts the transform on the
    synthetic outputs.
    \item Because $88.85\%$ of training events are $x = 0$, the
    empirical CDF has a vertical jump at $x=0$ of size $0.89$. All
    zero-valued training events get mapped to a single $z$ value
    (specifically, the value of $\Phi^{-1}$ at the midpoint of
    the jump, which sits well below zero on the $z$-axis).
    \item The diffusion model trained on this $z$-space sees an
    input distribution with a massive point mass and a thin
    continuous component on either side of it.
    ``$\eps$-prediction MSE on $z$-space'' is now an objective
    whose optimal solution is to output that same point mass,
    because that is where most of the probability is.
    \item At sampling time the model emits a $z$-distribution
    overwhelmingly clustered at the (negative) collapsed value,
    with minimal spread. The inverse transform $x =
    \hat{F}^{-1}(\Phi(z))$ then maps the cluster of $z$ values
    back to a cluster of $x$ values that all sit near zero ---
    and the heavy tails the inverse CDF was supposed to recover
    never get sampled because the model never produces $z$ values
    in the tail regions of $\Phi$.
\end{enumerate}

The mechanism is not subtle. The fix that v9 implements ---
adding a dequantization step that smears the $0.89$-mass point
evenly across a small interval before computing $\hat{F}$ ---
was already known in the normalizing-flow literature for exactly
the same reason (integer-valued image pixels create the same kind
of CDF discontinuity), and v9 recovers most of what v8 lost on
G1.

\paragraph{The promise: marginals from inverse CDF, joints from
diffusion.}
The reason the copula approach was attractive in the first place
is Sklar's theorem~\cite{sklar1959fonctions}: any joint
distribution decomposes into its marginals and a copula that
captures all the dependence structure between them. If we
transform every per-feature marginal to a standard normal $\Phi(0,
1)$ (via the per-feature empirical CDF followed by $\Phi^{-1}$),
the diffusion model only has to learn the \emph{copula} --- the
joint dependence in $z$-space, where every marginal is by
construction a unit Gaussian. At sampling time we invert: a
synthetic $z$ goes through $\hat{F}^{-1}(\Phi(z))$ and the heavy
tails of the original distribution come back ``for free'' from
the inverse CDF. Going into v8 we expected the synthetic kurtosis
to land in the $400$--$800$ range (i.e.\ within a factor of two
of real INTC's $755$); the marginal shape would be exact by
construction, and the only thing the diffusion model would have
to do is a job ($z$-space joint learning) for which it is
well-suited.

\paragraph{The failure: v8 z-space collapse.}
v8 is the naive copula attempt --- raw empirical CDF on each
feature, no dequantization. The diagnostic that exposes the
failure is the per-feature distribution of $z$-values that v8
actually \emph{emits} at sampling time, before the inverse-CDF
step. If the copula was working, the $z$-distribution should look
approximately $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. It does not
(Table~\ref{tab:v8_z_collapse}).

\subsection{Agent Implementation Details}
\label{app:agents_impl}

We chose the Avellaneda-Stoikov family of market-making agents
\cite{avellaneda2008high} because it is the most-studied parametric
model-MM strategy, the parameters are interpretable (inventory
aversion $\gamma$, liquidity intensity $\kappa$, time horizon $T$),
and it is small enough to extend with optional signals without
changing the optimal-control derivation. The four agents are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A0 --- Constant spread.} Posts $\pm 1$ tick around
    the prevailing mid at every event, no inventory adjustment, no
    signal. This is our null --- it should approximately collect the
    half-spread minus inventory PnL whenever its quotes are crossed.
    \item \textbf{A1 --- Avellaneda-Stoikov baseline.} Inventory-skewed
    reservation price $r = s - q\gamma\sigma^2(T - t)$ with
    half-spread $\delta = \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\sigma^2(T - t) +
    \tfrac{1}{\gamma}\ln(1 + \tfrac{\gamma}{\kappa})$, posted on each
    side. Calibrated $\gamma$ and $\kappa$ on the training set.
    \item \textbf{A2 --- A1 with order-flow-imbalance (OFI) skew.}
    Adds a short-horizon OFI term \cite{cont2014price} to the
    reservation price; quotes are leaned in the direction the OFI
    signal predicts the next-event return.
    \item \textbf{A3 --- A1 with VPIN-gated widening.} Volume-
    synchronized probability of informed trading
    \cite{easley2012flow}; when VPIN exceeds a threshold the agent
    widens its spread to compensate for adverse-selection risk.
\end{itemize}

The four agents above are deployed in a non-impact fill simulator:
at the start of each event the agent posts bid and ask limit orders;
if the next market event crosses the posted price (via a market
trade or a quote update that puts the new best on the wrong side of
the agent's quote), the agent gets filled at the posted price;
otherwise the orders are cancelled and re-posted at the next event.
Inventory and PnL are tracked event-by-event, with a forced position
close at the end of each tape. The Avellaneda-Stoikov parameters
($\gamma$, $\kappa$, $T$) are calibrated on the training window.
The bias structural to this setup --- recorded markets do not
respond to the agent's trades --- is the load-bearing reason
\S\ref{sec:agents} treats agent rankings as a consistency check
rather than as ground truth.

\subsection{Reproducer Commands}
\label{app:reproducer}
% Exact cluster commands (sbatch invocations) per checkpoint.
\paragraph{SPY chained training.}
SPY has roughly $10\times$ more events per day than TSLA, so a
single training epoch exceeds the cluster's 6-hour job time limit.
Our initial runs timed out before completing one epoch, and because
checkpoints were only saved at epoch boundaries, no recoverable
state was written. We fixed this by saving model state every
$10{,}000$ optimizer steps (in addition to the per-epoch save) and
submitting training as a chain of four consecutive 6-hour jobs per
architecture, each resuming from the most recent step-level
snapshot of the previous job with full optimizer and learning-rate
scheduler state restored. The combination keeps progress
recoverable on any dataset whose epoch length exceeds the
per-job time budget.
