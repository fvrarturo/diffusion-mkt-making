\section{Results --- The Three-Axis Tension}
\label{sec:results}
% ~6-7 pages now (Copula Story merged in as §5.7). Three protagonists
% (v2, v5, v9) tell the main story; full 20-checkpoint ablation explains
% WHY; the copula case study (§5.7) is the most pedagogically rich
% instance of "fixing one axis breaks another".

% ── §5.0 PREAMBLE — define the three-axis table up-front ───────────────────
% Goal: by end of page 1 of §5, the reader can already see the central
% tension: no architecture wins all three axes. Everything that follows is
% "why and how".

We trained twenty diffusion-model variants on INTC limit order book data
(Section~\ref{sec:checkpoints}), and evaluated each one on three independent
quality axes recapped from Section~\ref{sec:eval_framework}: distributional
realism (\textbf{G1}, sixteen stylized-fact pass/fail checks following
\cite{cont2001stylized}), temporal pattern transferability
(\textbf{predictive score}, an LSTM trained on synthetic data and tested
on real, MAE expressed as a multiple of the market-replay baseline,
following \cite{berti2025trades}), and multivariate state-space coverage
(\textbf{PCA convex-hull intersection} with the real per-tape feature
distribution).

When we put all twenty checkpoints into the same comparison frame
(Table~\ref{tab:multi_axis}, Figure~\ref{fig:hero_three_axis}), the pattern
that emerges is the central empirical finding of this report: \textbf{no
architecture optimizes all three axes simultaneously}. The model that
matches real INTC's heavy tails best (v5, kurtosis $1257$ vs.\ real $755$)
sits in the middle of the predictive-score ranking; the model that produces
the most realistic trade fraction (v9, $0.049$ vs.\ real $0.048$) sits at
the bottom of the predictive-score ranking ($106\times$ replay) despite
covering the broadest PCA region of the three; and the architectures that
dominate predictive transferability (v3\_e9\_noclip, $4\times$ replay; the
single best of all twenty) cover \emph{zero percent} of real's PCA hull.
The rest of \S\ref{sec:results} unpacks what each axis measures, where the
twenty checkpoints land on it, and why the rankings disagree across axes.
\S\ref{sec:res_anticorrelation} confirms that the disagreement is
systematic rather than cherry-picked, and \S\ref{sec:copula_story} works
through the copula case study, the single architectural family in which a
sequence of three small fixes moves all three axes at once and makes the
mechanism easy to read.

\subsection{Three Representative Generators}
\label{sec:three_protagonists}

To make the per-axis subsections readable, we anchor the rest of
\S\ref{sec:results} on three protagonists drawn from architecturally
orthogonal families. Each one is the best generator we built within its
family, and each one occupies a different ``corner'' of the three-axis
space.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{v2 --- the baseline} ($\eps$-prediction, FiLM
    conditioning, z-score normalization). Closest direct fine-tune of the
    TRADES backbone \cite{berti2025trades} on our preprocessed INTC tapes.
    Lands at G1 $= 12/15$, predictive $= 14.2\times$ replay, PCA
    $= 58.6\%$, kurtosis $= 122$ (against real $755$), trade fraction
    $= 0.558$ (against real $0.048$). \textbf{Wins predictive
    transferability among our three protagonists; weakest tails.}
    \item \textbf{v5 --- the heavy-tail champion} ($v$-prediction,
    AdaLN-Zero conditioning, z-score). G1 $= 13/15$, predictive
    $= 10.7\times$, PCA $= 46.1\%$, kurtosis $= 1257$ (overshoots real),
    trade fraction $= 0.552$. \textbf{Highest kurtosis of any generator;
    pays for it with weaker PCA coverage and a degraded trade-sign
    autocorrelation structure that \S\ref{sec:res_g1} exposes.}
    \item \textbf{v9 --- the distributional realist} ($\eps$-prediction,
    FiLM, copula transform with dequantization on the marginals).
    G1 $= 12/15$, predictive $= 106.4\times$ replay, PCA $= 71.4\%$,
    kurtosis $= 129$, trade fraction $= 0.049$ (essentially exact
    match). \textbf{Wins trade-fraction realism and PCA coverage among
    our three; the predictive-score collapse is a structural cost of the
    copula round-trip that \S\ref{sec:copula_story} explains in
    detail.}
\end{itemize}

The three rankings do not agree. v2 wins predictive but is the weakest
on distributional shape. v5 wins on the single most-cited stylized fact
(kurtosis), but its $v$-prediction objective does not translate into
better state-space coverage or temporal transferability. v9 wins on the
quantities a regulator might ask about (trade fraction, marginal shape,
PCA coverage) but is so dominated by copula round-trip noise that the
LSTM trained on its output is two orders of magnitude worse than market
replay. The three protagonists thus form a small Pareto frontier: each
is dominated by another on at least one axis, none is dominated on all.
Figure~\ref{fig:rank_bump} makes this visible across all twenty
checkpoints --- the line for each model crosses the others as you move
from G1 to predictive to PCA, and no line stays at the top across all
three columns.

\subsection{Distributional Realism Results}
\label{sec:res_g1}

The G1 suite is fifteen pass/fail checks of stylized facts in the
\cite{cont2001stylized} family, plus one consistency check (no crossed
books in the decoded synthetic stream). On the headline pass count, six
of the twenty checkpoints reach $13/15$ (v3\_e9, v4, v4\_noclip, v5, v7,
v7\_b), eight reach $12/15$, and the bottom of the table is the
``noclip'' family --- v3p5\_noclip at $5/15$, v3\_e9\_noclip at $6/15$,
v2\_noclip at $7/15$. We return to the noclip pattern in
\S\ref{sec:res_predictive} because it is the most informative single
trade-off in the whole study.

The pass count is a coarse measure that hides the per-check magnitudes,
so we also report the per-row numerical view in
Figure~\ref{fig:g1_heatmap}: each cell is the actual synthetic value of
that statistic, colored by how far it is from the real-data reference
\emph{relative to the other nineteen models on the same row}. A row is
green where the synthetic value is closest to real among the twenty,
red where it is the furthest; this normalization is per-row so a
Wasserstein distance at $10^{-6}$ scale and a kurtosis at $10^{3}$ scale
are both readable.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../results/report_figures/fig_2_1_g1_numerical_heatmap.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{G1 stylized-facts matrix --- synth values with
    cross-sectional error gradient.} Fifteen rows are the G1 checks
    (the ``Conditional moments'' check is dropped because its validator
    emits a categorical label rather than a numeric value). Twenty

Two findings dominate Figure~\ref{fig:g1_heatmap}. First, the
\textbf{tail-shape row} (return excess kurtosis, real $755$) is the
single highest-variance row: v5 sits at $1257$ (overshoot), v10\_cascade
at $4124$ (extreme overshoot driven by a tiny $\sigma\!\approx\!10^{-6}$
denominator), the central cluster (v3, v4, v6, v7, v9) at $\sim 70$--$190$,
and the noclip family at $0$--$4$ (effectively no excess kurtosis ---
the model collapsed to a near-Gaussian return distribution).
v8's kurtosis of $2.6$ is the most dramatic collapse and the launch
point for the copula case study in \S\ref{sec:copula_story}.

Second, the \textbf{trade-fraction row} (real $0.048$, i.e.\ $\sim 4.8\%$
of all events are trades; the remaining $95\%$ are quote updates) shows
that almost every generator over-emits trades by an order of magnitude
or more --- v2 sits at $0.558$, v3, v4, v6 at $0.45$--$0.78$, v5 at
$0.55$. Only four checkpoints land within $2\times$ of real:
\textbf{v9 at $0.049$} (essentially exact), v9\_b at $0.032$, v2\_remapped
at $0.045$, and v7\_b at $0.156$. The first three of those are also the
three checkpoints with the worst predictive scores in
\S\ref{sec:res_predictive}; the fourth is the only ``zero-extra-trade''
generator from the EDM family. We pull this thread further in
Figure~\ref{fig:trade_fraction}, which sorts all twenty by distance from
real's trade fraction and color-codes by distance.

\begin{figure}[h!]

    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{../results/report_figures/fig_2_4_trade_fraction.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Trade fraction across the twenty INTC generators.}
    Dashed line marks real's $0.0477$. Bars colored by distance from

A complementary view of return-tail behavior is the cumulative tail
probability at fixed $z$-thresholds (Figure~\ref{fig:return_tail}). For
the three protagonists, real lies above all three at every threshold ---
i.e.\ all three under-estimate the probability of large absolute
returns at the $|z|=2,3,4$ standard-deviation marks --- but the gap
narrows for v9 and v5 relative to v2. This says that the kurtosis
overshoot in v5 and the trade-fraction-aligned v9 still produce
\emph{lighter} tails than real, just by less. v2's curve drops fastest:
even though its overall G1 pass count is only one short of v5, it is
the protagonist that loses the tail-probability comparison most
visibly, consistent with v2's role as the conservative ``mode-covering''
$\eps$-prediction baseline.

\subsection{Temporal Transferability Results}
\label{sec:res_predictive}

The predictive score follows the TRADES protocol
\cite{berti2025trades}: train an LSTM on synthetic windows, test on a
held-out real window, report the ratio of test MAE to a market-replay
baseline (an LSTM trained on real data). A perfect generator would land
at $1.0$ --- the LSTM learns the same dynamics from synth as from real.
Higher is worse; below the baseline (i.e.\ a synth-trained LSTM that
\emph{outperforms} a real-trained one) is in principle possible but
should not happen on a well-specified test split.

The headline ranking surprised us, and is the main reason
\S\ref{sec:res_anticorrelation} ends where it ends.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{../results/report_figures/fig_3_1_predictive_score.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Predictive score across the twenty INTC
    checkpoints, log scale.} Test MAE relative to market-replay
    baseline ($1.0\times$, dashed). Lower is closer to the temporal
    structure of the real data; higher means the LSTM trained on synth
    transfers worse to real than an LSTM trained on real itself. The
    three best generators on this axis (v3\_e9\_noclip $4.0\times$,
    v2\_noclip $8.2\times$, v5 $10.7\times$) are also among the worst
    on G1 (v3\_e9\_noclip and v2\_noclip both pass fewer than half of
    the G1 checks). The five worst on this axis (v2\_remapped at
    $266\times$, v8\_b $209\times$, v11 $130\times$, v8 $117\times$,
    v9 $106\times$) are the four copula-family generators plus the
    Spearman-remapped variant of v2.}
    \label{fig:predictive_bar}
\end{figure}

The single most informative pair is v9 vs.\ v3\_e9\_noclip. v9 has the
\emph{best} marginal-distribution match in our entire study (kurtosis
$129$, trade fraction $0.049$, PCA $71.4\%$) and lands at predictive
$=106.4\times$. v3\_e9\_noclip is a checkpoint we wrote off as broken
in our internal tracking: it passes only $6/15$ G1 checks, its return
distribution has near-zero excess kurtosis ($-0.43$, the model has
collapsed to a smooth-tailed Gaussian), and its synthetic returns are
roughly $43\times$ wider in standard deviation than real INTC's
($\sigma_\text{synth} \approx 9.4 \times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $\sigma_\text{real}
\approx 2.2 \times 10^{-5}$). Yet it produces the best predictive
score in the study at $4.0\times$ replay --- twenty-six times better
than v9. Figure~\ref{fig:pred_vs_scale} pulls this contrast onto a
single scatter.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{../results/report_figures/fig_3_3_predictive_vs_scale.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Predictive score vs.\ synthetic return-scale ratio
    (synth std / real std), both log.} If the predictive LSTM cared
    primarily about getting return magnitudes right, the relationship
    would be monotone with a minimum at $1.0$ on the x-axis. It is
    not. The cluster of scale-matched models near $\sigma_\text{synth}
    \approx \sigma_\text{real}$ (v2, v3, v3p5, v4, v5, v6, v7, v7\_b)
    spans an entire order of magnitude on the y-axis ($10$--$36\times$
    replay); the scale-exploded noclip models at $\sigma_\text{synth}
    \approx 40\times \sigma_\text{real}$ sit at the \emph{bottom} of
    the y-axis ($4$--$14\times$); and the copula family at
    $\sigma_\text{synth} \approx 0.6\times \sigma_\text{real}$ sits at
    the \emph{top} ($28$--$210\times$). Scale matching is neither
    necessary nor sufficient for temporal transferability.}
    \label{fig:pred_vs_scale}
\end{figure}

What the LSTM is picking up is not absolute return magnitude --- it is
the per-event ordering and sign-correlation structure of the return
sequence. Once we look through that lens the noclip dominance has a
mechanical explanation: removing the $x_0$ output-clip lets the model
emit return-sign sequences with realistic per-event variation, even
though the marginal scale is wildly off; the copula family, by contrast,
runs the per-feature distribution through an empirical-CDF round-trip
that is essentially correct on marginals and Spearman ranks but
introduces enough per-event noise to wash out the predictable structure
the LSTM relies on. We exhibit this directly in the v2\_remapped
cautionary tale in \S\ref{sec:copula_lesson}: post-hoc Spearman remapping
of v2 preserves rank correlations \emph{exactly} and pushes predictive
score from v2's $14\times$ to v2\_remapped's $266\times$, a near-twentyfold
degradation purely from the rank-preserving shape change.

\subsection{Multivariate Coverage Results}
\label{sec:res_pca}

The PCA-coverage axis asks a different question from G1 and predictive
score. We compute one feature vector per stitched 2,560-event tape (mean
and std of each of the eight canonical features), z-score using the real
training set's per-feature statistics, fit a 2-D PCA on the real tapes,
project the synthetic tapes through the same PCA, and report the area
of the intersection between the real convex hull and the synthetic
convex hull, divided by the area of the real hull. A perfect generator
matches real on this axis at $100\%$; a generator that produces tapes
too tight or too wide to overlap with real lands at $0\%$.

The fifteen models with finite (non-degenerate) projections sort
into three regimes (Table~\ref{tab:pca_coverage_top}): the
\emph{coverage-dominant} models that wrap around the real region with
hulls one to two times bigger than real (v8, v10\_cascade), the
\emph{coverage-realistic} models whose hulls are proportionate to
real's $25.2$ unit-area (v9, v9\_b, v4\_noclip, v3\_e9, v7, v2, v4),
and the \emph{coverage-thin} models whose hulls fit inside real's region
without filling it (v6, v3\_e19, v3p5, v5, v7\_b, v2\_remapped). Three
of the noclip checkpoints (v2\_noclip, v3\_e9\_noclip, v3p5\_noclip)
land at $0\%$ coverage \emph{despite producing the largest hulls of all}
(synth hulls between $5{,}000$ and $12{,}000$ units of area, against
real's $25.2$): their synthetic projections are flung so far outside
the real region that the intersection collapses to nothing. v8\_b and
v11 are degenerate enough that the PCA coverage is undefined (their
synth std on at least one feature is exactly zero).

The two findings noted under Table~\ref{tab:pca_coverage_top} are the
ones we will return to in \S\ref{sec:res_anticorrelation}. Coverage is a
useful diagnostic but it is not a substitute for marginal realism: v8's
$85\%$ coverage comes with G1 $= 8/15$ and a kurtosis of $2.6$
(against real $755$), and the two best-PCA models in our entire study
are also the two whose marginals collapsed most severely. Conversely,
the noclip family --- which dominated predictive transferability in
\S\ref{sec:res_predictive} --- is the worst performer here, by a wide
margin. The pattern that wins on one axis loses on another.
