\begin{abstract}
We trained a family of diffusion models to generate synthetic limit
order book data --- the event-by-event record of buy and sell orders
that drives a stock price --- and asked which architectural choices
make a model better at the job. The motivation is practical: trading-
strategy backtests on historical data are biased because the recorded
market does not react to the strategy under test, and synthetic
generators are the standard fix, but the literature has not been clear
about what ``better generation'' means in this setting. We trained
twenty diffusion variants on Intel (INTC) order-book data, varying
five architectural design choices, and evaluated each one along three
independent quality axes: how well per-feature distributions match
real data, how useful the synthetic data is for training a downstream
prediction model, and how well the model spans the full range of
market conditions seen historically. We also ran four reference market-
making strategies against each generator and re-ran the full pipeline
on Tesla (TSLA) and the S\&P 500 ETF (SPY) to test cross-stock
generalization. The central finding is that no architectural choice we
tested wins on all three quality axes at once --- the model that best
matches per-feature distributions is not the one most useful for
downstream training, which is not the one with the broadest market-
state coverage. The trade-off is partly mechanical (a single squared-
error training criterion cannot optimize all three at the same time on
heavy-tailed data) and partly stock-specific (it is sharper on INTC
than on TSLA). For practitioners of synthetic LOB data the implication
is that the right model depends on which axis the downstream task
rewards, and we recommend evaluating against all three before
committing.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{The Backtesting Problem}
\label{sec:backtest_problem}

The standard way to evaluate a trading strategy is to replay it against
recorded historical market data. The problem with the standard way ---
already familiar inside the market-microstructure literature and
re-emphasized recently by \cite{berti2025trades} --- is that recorded
markets are \emph{non-responsive}: the order book is what it was on
that day, regardless of what the strategy under test would have done
to it. A market-making agent that posts an aggressive quote and gets
filled in the replay was not actually competing for that fill; the
counterparty trade happens exactly as it did historically. The next
quote update is the same. So is the realized adverse selection. Any
market-impact-bearing strategy gets credit it would not have received
in production, which is the most common reason a backtest looks better
than the live strategy. The methodological response over the last
decade has been to generate synthetic market data on which strategies
can be tested under counterfactual conditions, and the live question
in that line of work is which generator produces synthetic LOB streams
realistic enough to be useful for that purpose.

\subsection{Why Diffusion Models for LOBs}
\label{sec:why_diffusion}

The first wave of generative LOB models was built on
GANs~\cite{li2020generating, coletta2021towards, coletta2022learning},
which produced LOB-shaped output but suffered from the mode-collapse
failure mode that GANs exhibit on heavy-tailed data: the discriminator
is easiest to fool by emitting the bulk of the distribution and
ignoring the tails, and the generator obliges. For LOB data, where
the most consequential events (large trades, regime breaks) live in
the tails, mode collapse is the wrong failure mode. Denoising diffusion
probabilistic models~\cite{ho2020denoising} replace the
adversarial dynamic with an explicit denoising objective, train stably
without mode-collapse pathology, and admit flexible conditioning ---
all properties that fit the LOB-generation problem. The TRADES
work~\cite{berti2025trades} is the first applied diffusion architecture
for LOB simulation and is the methodological anchor for our project:
we adopt their transformer denoiser, their event-tensor representation,
and their predictive-score evaluation protocol, and we ask what happens
when the architectural design choices around that backbone are varied
systematically.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This report makes five contributions, each of which we treat as a
finding rather than a method claim --- the diffusion machinery itself
is not new, but what the machinery does on this kind of data, when
varied systematically, is.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A systematic ablation of \textbf{twenty diffusion-model
    checkpoints} across five orthogonal architectural axes (prediction
    target, conditioning, preconditioning, data transform, loss
    weighting), all sharing the TRADES backbone~\cite{berti2025trades}
    and trained on the same INTC corpus.
    \item Identification of a \textbf{three-axis tension} between
    distributional realism, temporal transferability, and multivariate
    state-space coverage --- no checkpoint we trained wins all three,
    and the per-axis rankings are systematically anti-correlated across
    architecture families.
    \item A mechanistic account of \emph{why} the tension arises:
    per-element MSE on heavy-tailed multivariate targets cannot
    simultaneously match per-feature marginals and joint coverage, and
    rank-preserving marginal fixes scramble the per-event values that
    the predictive-transferability evaluation rewards.
    \item Stress-testing of the four-agent Avellaneda-Stoikov family
    against each generator. Agent-ranking metrics
    decouple from the three primary quality axes, but the
    historical-replay reference is itself biased (non-responsive); we
    therefore report the empirical patterns that survive across the
    spread of generator-induced rankings rather than treating any
    single generator's ranking as ground truth.
    \item \textbf{Cross-asset validation} on INTC (large tick), TSLA
    (small tick), and SPY (index ETF, in flight at submission), which
    qualifies the structural-vs-microstructure-specific reading of the
    three-axis tension --- on TSLA the tension partially dissolves, so
    the picture from INTC alone overstates how universal the trade-off
    is.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limit Order Book Mechanics}
\label{sec:lob_mechanics}

A limit order book (LOB) is the matching-engine view of a security: a
price-time-priority queue of resting limit orders, with the buy side
(\emph{bids}) ranked by descending price and the sell side (\emph{asks})
by ascending price. The highest bid and lowest ask form the
\emph{inside quote}; their average is the \emph{midprice} and their
difference is the \emph{spread}. Two event types drive the book
forward: quote updates (a limit order is submitted, cancelled, or has a
queue position change) and trades (a marketable order is matched
against resting liquidity and consumes it). INTC, the primary ticker
in this study, trades at roughly \$30 against a one-cent tick, which
makes one tick worth $\sim$3.3~bps and pins the spread at exactly one
tick essentially all the time. As a direct consequence, the inside
quote is stable across the vast majority of events --- in our training
window roughly $89\%$ of events leave the midprice unchanged, and the
per-event return is therefore a heavily zero-inflated quantity rather
than a continuous Gaussian-like one. This is the structural property
that becomes load-bearing in \S\ref{sec:copula_story}; we flag it here
so the reader recognizes it when it returns.

\subsection{Data Source and Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:data_source}

The raw data is NYSE TAQ accessed via WRDS, providing nanosecond-resolution
quote and trade messages from which we reconstruct the top-of-book LOB
state stream using a LOBSTER-style pipeline. We do not model the full
multi-level book; the diffusion model operates on an eight-feature
event tensor that captures the inside quote and the most recent trade:
$\Delta t$ (time gap to the previous event, log-scaled), $\text{event\_type}$
(categorical, one-hot encoded), $\text{bid\_dist}$ and $\text{ask\_dist}$
(best-level distance from the prevailing midprice, in ticks),
$\text{bid\_sz}$ and $\text{ask\_sz}$ (top-of-book queue sizes),
$\text{trade\_sz}$ and $\text{trade\_sign}$ (zero unless the event is a
trade), and $\text{mid\_return}$ (the relative midprice change driven
by the event). All numeric features are $z$-scored using statistics fit
on the training window only. The diffusion model's input/output unit is
a $256$-event non-overlapping window; ten consecutive windows are
stitched at sampling time into a $2{,}560$-event pseudo-day, which is
the unit on which all evaluation in \S\ref{sec:results} is
computed. Before we move on to the splits, two pieces of empirical
correlation structure are worth pre-flagging because they recur
load-bearing later in the report: $\text{bid\_dist}$ and
$\text{ask\_dist}$ are mechanically anti-correlated (the inside quote
moves rather than each side moving independently), and the
order-flow-imbalance signal that A2 trades on in \S\ref{sec:agent_design}
predicts $\text{mid\_return}$ at a Spearman correlation of only
$\sim$0.07~\cite{cont2014price} --- a real but faint signal that is
straightforward to scramble.

\subsection{Train / Validation / Holdout Split and Regime Labelling}
\label{sec:splits}

We use a temporal $60 / 10 / 20$ day split for INTC: the first sixty
trading days train the diffusion model, the next ten pick checkpoints
and tune hyperparameters, and the final twenty are the holdout window
that produces every number reported in \S\ref{sec:results},
\S\ref{sec:agents}, and \S\ref{sec:cross_asset}. The holdout is never
seen during training or model selection. Each day in the corpus is
additionally tagged with one of four regime labels --- \emph{base},
\emph{high\_vol} (top quartile of realized variance), \emph{toxic} (top
quartile of VPIN~\cite{easley2012flow}), and \emph{thin} (bottom
quartile of trade volume) --- and that label is the conditioning vector
consumed by the AdaLN-Zero modulation block of \S\ref{sec:conditioning}.
Regime conditioning is what lets the generator produce stress days on
demand rather than only the average day; without it a downstream user
who wants a high-volatility scenario would have to keep sampling and
filtering. The TSLA and SPY corpora used in
\S\ref{sec:cross_asset} follow the same split convention but with
ticker-specific regime thresholds (the absolute volatility level that
counts as ``high\_vol'' on SPY is much lower than on INTC).

\subsection{Stylized Facts of Financial Returns}
\label{sec:stylized_facts}

The classical Cont (2001) checklist of financial-return stylized
facts~\cite{cont2001stylized} is the empirical anchor against which the
G1 distributional realism axis (\S\ref{sec:eval_g1}) is defined. Six
qualitative facts matter for the present discussion, each of which we
report INTC-specific numbers for so that comparisons in
\S\ref{sec:res_g1} are easy to read. (i) \emph{Heavy tails:} the
realized kurtosis of INTC per-event mid-returns is $\approx 755$,
versus the Gaussian value of $3$. (ii) \emph{Volatility clustering:}
the autocorrelation function of $|\text{mid\_return}|$ decays slowly
and remains positive across many lags, while (iii) the autocorrelation
of $\text{mid\_return}$ itself is statistically zero at lag one
(consistent with the no-arbitrage prediction). (iv) \emph{Trade-sign
persistence:} the lag-1 autocorrelation of $\text{trade\_sign}$ is
positive (order-flow is splitted across many child orders).
(v) \emph{Volume-volatility correlation:} per-tape average trade size
is positively correlated with per-tape realized volatility.
(vi) \emph{Leverage effect:} past returns are negatively correlated
with future volatility, though weakly at the per-event scale we
operate on. These six high-level facts are operationalized into the
sixteen pass/fail checks of the G1 matrix (Appendix~\ref{app:g1_matrix})
with explicit tolerances. Figure~\ref{fig:real_stylized_facts}
visualizes the same six facts on a 10-day val subset and serves as the
pedagogical reference image for what every generator in
\S\ref{sec:results} is trying to reproduce.\footnote{The two real-INTC
reference numbers we cite throughout this report come from two sample
windows. The kurtosis and tail-shape numbers (e.g.\ kurt $\approx 755$)
are computed on the 10-day val subset shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:real_stylized_facts}; the per-feature aggregate
statistics in our deep diagnostic
(\texttt{results/deep\_diagnostic/per\_feature\_stats.csv}) are
computed on the full $\sim$40M-event val window and report frac\_zero
$= 0.8885$ (used in \S\ref{sec:copula_zero_inflation}). Standard
deviations agree across windows ($\approx 2.2\times 10^{-5}$);
kurtosis and frac\_zero are sensitive to which window is averaged
because the heavy tail and the zero-mass are jointly driven by a small
fraction of high-activity days.}

\subsection{Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models}
\label{sec:ddpm}

A denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM)~\cite{ho2020denoising}
defines a forward Markov chain that gradually corrupts a clean sample
$\mathbf{x}_0$ into Gaussian noise $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},
\mathbf{I})$ over $T$ steps with a fixed variance schedule
$\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$, and a learned reverse chain $p_\theta$ that
denoises $\mathbf{x}_T$ back to a sample from the data distribution.
With $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$,
the forward marginal at step $t$ is
$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon$
with $\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$, and the
training loss in the standard $\epsilon$-prediction
parameterization is
$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol\epsilon}
\bigl[\,\lVert\boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c})\rVert_2^2\,\bigr]$,
i.e.\ per-element MSE on the noise prediction conditioned on optional
side information $\mathbf{c}$ (in our case a regime label). We use
$T=1000$ steps with the cosine variance schedule of
\cite{nichol2021improved} and a transformer denoiser following
TRADES~\cite{berti2025trades}. All twenty checkpoints share this
backbone; they differ only along the five design axes of
\S\ref{sec:design_space}.

\subsection{The Architectural Design Space}
\label{sec:design_space}

We organize the design space around five axes that each control a
different piece of the training/sampling pipeline: the prediction target
(what the network regresses against), the conditioning mechanism (how the
regime label enters), the preconditioning (how loss magnitude is
normalized across noise levels), the data transform (what space we
diffuse in), and the loss weighting (which timesteps the gradient is
allowed to favor). The remainder of \S\ref{sec:design_space} introduces
each axis in order, and \S\ref{sec:checkpoints} reports which of the
$2{\times}2{\times}2{\times}3{\times}2 = 48$ possible cells we actually
trained.

\subsubsection{Prediction Target: $\eps$ vs $v$}
\label{sec:pred_target}

The standard $\eps$-prediction parameterization of \cite{ho2020denoising}
asks the network to regress the noise added at step $t$. Because the
loss is per-element MSE and is dominated by the high-noise regime where
$\boldsymbol\epsilon$ already overwhelms signal, the gradient is
mode-covering: it converges to a denoising function that averages over
plausible reconstructions and undershoots tail magnitudes. The
\emph{velocity} parameterization $v$ of \cite{salimans2022progressive}
re-targets the network onto
$\mathbf{v}_t \equiv \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0$,
which makes loss variance more uniform across noise levels and gives the
gradient at low noise more leverage to commit to the actual signal.
Empirically this sharpens marginal tails (\S\ref{sec:res_g1}) but, as
\S\ref{sec:res_pca} reports, the per-element nature of the criterion
means the model commits to extreme values without coordinating those
extremes across features. Both targets are end-to-end MSE
parameterizations; the difference is which quantity the per-element
gradient is asked to drive to zero.

\subsubsection{Conditioning: FiLM vs AdaLN-Zero}
\label{sec:conditioning}

The regime label and timestep embedding need to enter every transformer
block. FiLM~\cite{perez2018film} computes per-block scale and shift
parameters $(\gamma_\ell, \beta_\ell)$ from the conditioning vector and
applies $\gamma_\ell\odot\mathbf{h}_\ell + \beta_\ell$ to the block's
hidden state --- four modulation parameters per block. The AdaLN-Zero
mechanism of the diffusion transformer (DiT)~\cite{peebles2023scalable}
instead replaces the block's LayerNorm with an adaptive one whose
scale/shift \emph{and} a residual gate are produced by the conditioning
vector, with the gate initialized to zero so each block begins as the
identity --- six modulation parameters per block, plus a stable warm-up
trajectory. We tested both. FiLM-conditioned variants in our Phase F
runs exhibited a modulation-collapse failure mode in which $\gamma$
saturated near $1$ and $\beta$ near $0$, effectively disabling the
conditioning; AdaLN-Zero did not. All twenty checkpoints reported in
\S\ref{sec:results} use AdaLN-Zero.

\subsubsection{Preconditioning: EDM (Karras et al.\ 2022)}
\label{sec:edm}

The EDM framework of \cite{karras2022elucidating} reframes diffusion
training around a continuous noise scale $\sigma$ and wraps the
denoising network with $\sigma$-dependent input, output, skip, and noise
scalings $(c_{\text{in}}, c_{\text{out}}, c_{\text{skip}}, c_{\text{noise}})$
chosen so that the network's effective input and target magnitudes are
order-unity at every $\sigma$. Without preconditioning the network has
to learn very different output magnitudes at low and high noise; with
preconditioning the operating range is comfortable everywhere and the
training loss landscape becomes more isotropic across timesteps. The
parameterization has one free hyperparameter, $\sigma_{\text{data}}$,
which is the assumed scale of the clean signal; we swept
$\sigma_{\text{data}} \in \{0.5, 1.0, 1.5\}$ across the v6 / v7 / v7\_b
checkpoints (Appendix~\ref{app:checkpoints}).

\subsubsection{Data Transform: z-score vs Copula}
\label{sec:copula_transform}

The default transform is per-feature $z$-scoring (subtract training-set
mean, divide by training-set standard deviation), which preserves the
relative structure of each feature but leaves the diffusion model
responsible for learning the marginal shape from the data. The copula
construction is an alternative based on Sklar's
theorem~\cite{sklar1959fonctions}: estimate the empirical CDF
$\widehat{F}_i$ of each feature from training data, transform every
sample as $z_i = \Phi^{-1}(\widehat{F}_i(x_i))$, and diffuse on $z$
rather than on $x$. By construction the marginals of $z$ are exactly
$\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, so the diffusion model only has to learn the
\emph{copula} (the dependence structure); the marginal shape is
restored at sampling time by the inverse map
$x_i = \widehat{F}_i^{-1}(\Phi(z_i))$, which makes per-feature G1 checks
trivially correct by construction. The complication is that for
zero-inflated features (e.g.\ INTC's $\sim 89\%$ zero-return events
documented in \S\ref{sec:stylized_facts}) $\widehat{F}_i$ has a step
discontinuity, so $\Phi^{-1}\circ\widehat{F}_i$ collapses an entire
$z$-band to a single value and breaks the diffusion target.
Dequantization~\cite{theis2016note} fixes this by adding small uniform
noise inside the discontinuity before the CDF is applied; we use this
construction in the v9 family. \S\ref{sec:copula_story} works through
the v8 (no dequant) $\to$ v9 (with dequant) progression as a case study
of how a single architectural axis trades quality across all three
evaluation axes at once.

\subsubsection{Loss Weighting: Min-SNR-$\gamma$ (Hang et al.\ 2023)}
\label{sec:min_snr}

The Min-SNR-$\gamma$ scheme~\cite{hang2023efficient} reweights the
per-step training loss by $\min(\mathrm{SNR}(t),\gamma)/\mathrm{SNR}(t)$,
which caps the contribution of low-noise (high-SNR) timesteps that
otherwise dominate the gradient and cause the model to overfit to
imperceptible high-frequency detail at the cost of mid-frequency
structure. The published default is $\gamma{=}5$. We swept
$\gamma\in\{1, 5\}$. The $\gamma{=}1$ setting over-corrects: it
downweights the low-noise regime so aggressively that the model fails to
learn the per-event detail required for any of our three evaluation
axes. We document this falsification in Appendix~\ref{app:phaseA}; all
twenty checkpoints reported in \S\ref{sec:results} use $\gamma{=}5$.

\subsection{The 20 Trained Checkpoints}
\label{sec:checkpoints}

A naive cross of the five axes gives forty-eight cells, more than we
could train under our compute budget. We instead trained twenty
checkpoints chosen along three logical arms. The \emph{scale-accurate
arm} (v1 -- v5, plus v6 / v7 / v7\_b) keeps the standard MSE-on-noise
recipe intact and varies prediction target, EDM preconditioning, and
$\sigma_{\text{data}}$. The \emph{scale-exploded arm} (v3\_e9\_noclip,
v3\_e25\_noclip, v3\_e10) drops gradient clipping and Min-SNR weighting
to expose how aggressive the network can become in the predictive
direction at the explicit cost of distributional realism --- this arm
is what populates the predictive-corner of \S\ref{sec:res_predictive}.
The \emph{copula arm} (v8, v8\_b, v9, v9\_b, v10, v11, v2\_remapped)
holds the backbone fixed and varies the data transform between
$z$-score, copula, and copula+dequantization, and sweeps
post-hoc remap variants of v2 to isolate which axis the copula moves
through. The full configuration matrix is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:checkpoints}; throughout \S\ref{sec:results} we lean
on three protagonists --- v2 (best G1), v5 (heaviest tails), v9
(highest PCA coverage) --- with the rest cited only when an ablation
makes the mechanism easier to read.

\subsection{Evaluation Framework: The Three Axes}
\label{sec:eval_framework}

A generator can fail a synthetic-LOB benchmark in qualitatively different
ways: it can match per-feature distributions but produce sequences whose
temporal structure breaks downstream models; it can produce sequences
that look temporally rich but only span a narrow region of the joint
state space; or it can do well on the per-tape average and badly on the
tail moments that actually matter for risk. We therefore evaluate each
checkpoint on three independent axes plus one consistency check.

\subsubsection{Distributional Realism (G1)}
\label{sec:eval_g1}

Sixteen stylized-fact checks following \cite{cont2001stylized}, plus a
small number of microstructure-specific additions (zero-return event
fraction, OFI distribution, trade fraction, conditional moments).
Each check is a pass / fail comparison of a synthetic statistic against
the corresponding real statistic, with tolerances calibrated against
the bootstrap interval of the same statistic computed on independent
real chunks. We report per-model pass count out of sixteen; the full
per-check matrix is given in Appendix~\ref{app:g1_matrix}.

\subsubsection{Temporal Pattern Transferability (Predictive Score)}
\label{sec:eval_predictive}

Following the TRADES protocol~\cite{berti2025trades}, we train a
two-layer LSTM on synthetic data to predict the next mid-price return
from the prior twenty events, evaluate it on a held-out real window,
and report the test MAE expressed as a multiple of the
train-on-real / test-on-real baseline. A score of $1.0$ means the
synthetic data is as useful as real data for training the LSTM;
$10.0$ means the LSTM trained on synthetic does ten times worse than
one trained on real. This is the standard ``train-synth, test-real''
methodology and answers the question: \emph{can a downstream sequence
model learn useful patterns from this synthetic data?}

\subsubsection{Multivariate Coverage (PCA Convex Hull)}
\label{sec:eval_pca}

Each 2{,}560-event tape is summarized by a fixed feature vector
(per-tape volatility, mean OFI, return skew and kurtosis, trade
fraction, and a small number of others); we fit a 2-D PCA on the real
tape feature vectors and project the synthetic tapes into the same
coordinates. Coverage is reported as the area of the intersection of
the synthetic convex hull with the real convex hull, divided by the
area of the real hull. A coverage of $100\%$ means synthetic spans the
joint state-space of real; $0\%$ means synthetic lives entirely outside
real's state-space.

\subsubsection{Agent-Based Ranking ($\rdiff$) and Why It Decouples}
\label{sec:eval_agents}

We additionally measure $\rdiff$, the Spearman rank correlation between
synthetic-data and historical-data terminal-PnL rankings of the
Avellaneda-Stoikov family of market makers we describe in
\S\ref{sec:agent_design} (specifically agents A0--A3 from
\cite{avellaneda2008high, cont2014price, easley2012flow}). $\rdiff$ is
intended as a top-level practical check: if a generator preserves
agent rankings, it is at least usable for downstream strategy
comparison even when it fails one of the three primary axes. We flag in
advance the central finding of \S\ref{sec:agents}: $\rdiff$ does not
align cleanly with G1, predictive, or PCA, but the historical reference
ranking it is benchmarked against is itself biased (replay does not
respond to the agent's trades), so we treat $\rdiff$ as a consistency
check and report what survives the spread, not as a fourth
quality axis.

\subsection{Three Representative Generators}
\label{sec:three_protagonists}

To make the per-axis subsections readable, we anchor the rest of
\S\ref{sec:results} on three protagonists drawn from architecturally
orthogonal families. Each one is the best generator we built within its
family, and each one occupies a different ``corner'' of the three-axis
space.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{v2 --- the baseline} ($\eps$-prediction, FiLM
    conditioning, z-score normalization). Closest direct fine-tune of the
    TRADES backbone \cite{berti2025trades} on our preprocessed INTC tapes.
    Lands at G1 $= 12/15$, predictive $= 14.2\times$ replay, PCA
    $= 58.6\%$, kurtosis $= 122$ (against real $755$), trade fraction
    $= 0.558$ (against real $0.048$). \textbf{Wins predictive
    transferability among our three protagonists; weakest tails.}
    \item \textbf{v5 --- the heavy-tail champion} ($v$-prediction,
    AdaLN-Zero conditioning, z-score). G1 $= 13/15$, predictive
    $= 10.7\times$, PCA $= 46.1\%$, kurtosis $= 1257$ (overshoots real),
    trade fraction $= 0.552$. \textbf{Highest kurtosis of any generator;
    pays for it with weaker PCA coverage and a degraded trade-sign
    autocorrelation structure that \S\ref{sec:res_g1} exposes.}
    \item \textbf{v9 --- the distributional realist} ($\eps$-prediction,
    FiLM, copula transform with dequantization on the marginals).
    G1 $= 12/15$, predictive $= 106.4\times$ replay, PCA $= 71.4\%$,
    kurtosis $= 129$, trade fraction $= 0.049$ (essentially exact
    match). \textbf{Wins trade-fraction realism and PCA coverage among
    our three; the predictive-score collapse is a structural cost of the
    copula round-trip that \S\ref{sec:copula_story} explains in
    detail.}
\end{itemize}

The three rankings do not agree. v2 wins predictive but is the weakest
on distributional shape. v5 wins on the single most-cited stylized fact
(kurtosis), but its $v$-prediction objective does not translate into
better state-space coverage or temporal transferability. v9 wins on the
quantities a regulator might ask about (trade fraction, marginal shape,
PCA coverage) but is so dominated by copula round-trip noise that the
LSTM trained on its output is two orders of magnitude worse than market
replay. The three protagonists thus form a small Pareto frontier: each
is dominated by another on at least one axis, none is dominated on all.
Figure~\ref{fig:rank_bump} makes this visible across all twenty
checkpoints --- the line for each model crosses the others as you move
from G1 to predictive to PCA, and no line stays at the top across all
three columns.

\subsection{Distributional Realism Results}
\label{sec:res_g1}

The G1 suite is fifteen pass/fail checks of stylized facts in the
\cite{cont2001stylized} family, plus one consistency check (no crossed
books in the decoded synthetic stream). On the headline pass count, six
of the twenty checkpoints reach $13/15$ (v3\_e9, v4, v4\_noclip, v5, v7,
v7\_b), eight reach $12/15$, and the bottom of the table is the
``noclip'' family --- v3p5\_noclip at $5/15$, v3\_e9\_noclip at $6/15$,
v2\_noclip at $7/15$. We return to the noclip pattern in
\S\ref{sec:res_predictive} because it is the most informative single
trade-off in the whole study.

The pass count is a coarse measure that hides the per-check magnitudes,
so we also report the per-row numerical view in
Figure~\ref{fig:g1_heatmap}: each cell is the actual synthetic value of
that statistic, colored by how far it is from the real-data reference
\emph{relative to the other nineteen models on the same row}. A row is
green where the synthetic value is closest to real among the twenty,
red where it is the furthest; this normalization is per-row so a
Wasserstein distance at $10^{-6}$ scale and a kurtosis at $10^{3}$ scale
are both readable.

\subsection{Temporal Transferability Results}
\label{sec:res_predictive}

The predictive score follows the TRADES protocol
\cite{berti2025trades}: train an LSTM on synthetic windows, test on a
held-out real window, report the ratio of test MAE to a market-replay
baseline (an LSTM trained on real data). A perfect generator would land
at $1.0$ --- the LSTM learns the same dynamics from synth as from real.
Higher is worse; below the baseline (i.e.\ a synth-trained LSTM that
\emph{outperforms} a real-trained one) is in principle possible but
should not happen on a well-specified test split.

The headline ranking surprised us, and is the main reason
\S\ref{sec:res_anticorrelation} ends where it ends.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{fig_2_3_return_tail.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Predictive score across the twenty INTC
    checkpoints, log scale.} Test MAE relative to market-replay
    baseline ($1.0\times$, dashed). Lower is closer to the temporal
    structure of the real data; higher means the LSTM trained on synth
    transfers worse to real than an LSTM trained on real itself. The
    three best generators on this axis (v3\_e9\_noclip $4.0\times$,
    v2\_noclip $8.2\times$, v5 $10.7\times$) are also among the worst
    on G1 (v3\_e9\_noclip and v2\_noclip both pass fewer than half of
    the G1 checks). The five worst on this axis (v2\_remapped at
    $266\times$, v8\_b $209\times$, v11 $130\times$, v8 $117\times$,
    v9 $106\times$) are the four copula-family generators plus the
    Spearman-remapped variant of v2.}
    \label{fig:predictive_bar}
\end{figure}

The single most informative pair is v9 vs.\ v3\_e9\_noclip. v9 has the
\emph{best} marginal-distribution match in our entire study (kurtosis
$129$, trade fraction $0.049$, PCA $71.4\%$) and lands at predictive
$=106.4\times$. v3\_e9\_noclip is a checkpoint we wrote off as broken
in our internal tracking: it passes only $6/15$ G1 checks, its return
distribution has near-zero excess kurtosis ($-0.43$, the model has
collapsed to a smooth-tailed Gaussian), and its synthetic returns are
roughly $43\times$ wider in standard deviation than real INTC's
($\sigma_\text{synth} \approx 9.4 \times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $\sigma_\text{real}
\approx 2.2 \times 10^{-5}$). Yet it produces the best predictive
score in the study at $4.0\times$ replay --- twenty-six times better
than v9. Figure~\ref{fig:pred_vs_scale} pulls this contrast onto a
single scatter.

    \centering

    \approx 40\times \sigma_\text{real}$ sit at the \emph{bottom} of
    the y-axis ($4$--$14\times$); and the copula family at
    $\sigma_\text{synth} \approx 0.6\times \sigma_\text{real}$ sits at
    the \emph{top} ($28$--$210\times$). Scale matching is neither
    necessary nor sufficient for temporal transferability.}
    \label{fig:pred_vs_scale}
\end{figure}

What the LSTM is picking up is not absolute return magnitude --- it is
the per-event ordering and sign-correlation structure of the return
sequence. Once we look through that lens the noclip dominance has a
mechanical explanation: removing the $x_0$ output-clip lets the model
emit return-sign sequences with realistic per-event variation, even
though the marginal scale is wildly off; the copula family, by contrast,
runs the per-feature distribution through an empirical-CDF round-trip
that is essentially correct on marginals and Spearman ranks but
introduces enough per-event noise to wash out the predictable structure
the LSTM relies on. We exhibit this directly in the v2\_remapped
cautionary tale in \S\ref{sec:copula_lesson}: post-hoc Spearman remapping
of v2 preserves rank correlations \emph{exactly} and pushes predictive
score from v2's $14\times$ to v2\_remapped's $266\times$, a near-twentyfold
degradation purely from the rank-preserving shape change.

\subsection{Multivariate Coverage Results}
\label{sec:res_pca}

The PCA-coverage axis asks a different question from G1 and predictive
score. We compute one feature vector per stitched 2,560-event tape (mean
and std of each of the eight canonical features), z-score using the real
training set's per-feature statistics, fit a 2-D PCA on the real tapes,
project the synthetic tapes through the same PCA, and report the area
of the intersection between the real convex hull and the synthetic
convex hull, divided by the area of the real hull. A perfect generator
matches real on this axis at $100\%$; a generator that produces tapes
too tight or too wide to overlap with real lands at $0\%$.

The fifteen models with finite (non-degenerate) projections sort
into three regimes (Table~\ref{tab:pca_coverage_top}): the
\emph{coverage-dominant} models that wrap around the real region with
hulls one to two times bigger than real (v8, v10\_cascade), the
\emph{coverage-realistic} models whose hulls are proportionate to
real's $25.2$ unit-area (v9, v9\_b, v4\_noclip, v3\_e9, v7, v2, v4),
and the \emph{coverage-thin} models whose hulls fit inside real's region
without filling it (v6, v3\_e19, v3p5, v5, v7\_b, v2\_remapped). Three
of the noclip checkpoints (v2\_noclip, v3\_e9\_noclip, v3p5\_noclip)
land at $0\%$ coverage \emph{despite producing the largest hulls of all}
(synth hulls between $5{,}000$ and $12{,}000$ units of area, against
real's $25.2$): their synthetic projections are flung so far outside
the real region that the intersection collapses to nothing. v8\_b and
v11 are degenerate enough that the PCA coverage is undefined (their
synth std on at least one feature is exactly zero).

    so far past real that the spatial overlap vanishes even as the
    spatial \emph{extent} explodes.}
    \label{tab:pca_coverage_top}
\end{table}

The two findings noted under Table~\ref{tab:pca_coverage_top} are the
ones we will return to in \S\ref{sec:res_anticorrelation}. Coverage is a
useful diagnostic but it is not a substitute for marginal realism: v8's
$85\%$ coverage comes with G1 $= 8/15$ and a kurtosis of $2.6$
(against real $755$), and the two best-PCA models in our entire study
are also the two whose marginals collapsed most severely. Conversely,
the noclip family --- which dominated predictive transferability in
\S\ref{sec:res_predictive} --- is the worst performer here, by a wide
margin. The pattern that wins on one axis loses on another.

the best predictive scores all over-emit trades by 10--20$\times$.

extra dequantization noise that lets the model recover correct trade
fractions also scrambles the per-event temporal structure the LSTM
relies on. \S\ref{sec:copula_lesson} (the v2\_remapped tale) makes the

\subsection{Architectural Ablation: What Drives What}
\label{sec:res_ablation}

The Spearman matrix in \S\ref{sec:res_anticorrelation} shows
\emph{that} the architecture space partitions into families with
different per-axis strengths; this subsection asks \emph{which
individual architectural toggle pushes which axis}, with v2/v5/v9 as
the three reference points. We organize the ablation as two walks
through the architecture graph --- v2 $\to$ v5 (the heavy-tail walk)
and v2 $\to$ v9 (the distributional-realist walk) --- and a third
``negative example'' walk to the noclip family to expose the most
informative trade-off in the study.

The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_walks} are averages over
matched checkpoint pairs that differ on exactly the toggle named in
each row. Appendix~\ref{app:ablation_full} gives the full six-toggle
$\times$ six-metric heatmap from which these numbers are extracted.

\paragraph{v2 $\to$ v5: the heavy-tail walk.}
v5 differs from v2 on two toggles --- prediction target ($\eps \to v$)
and conditioning (FiLM $\to$ AdaLN-Zero). The first row of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation_walks} says $\eps \to v$ on its own lowers
kurtosis by $\sim 30$ (mode-covering bias on bulk goes away) and
improves predictive by $\sim 30\times$ replay, with essentially no
effect on G1. The second row says FiLM $\to$ AdaLN does the opposite
on predictive ($+35\times$ replay, i.e.\ worse) but adds an enormous
$+1184$ to kurtosis --- AdaLN-Zero gives the model enough conditioning
bandwidth to commit to extreme samples, which is exactly what was
needed to overshoot real's kurtosis of $755$ to v5's $1257$. The net
effect of the two toggles together is the v5 we observe: better
kurtosis, marginally better predictive, slightly worse PCA, same G1.
The takeaway from this walk: \emph{tail behavior is dominated by
conditioning bandwidth, not by prediction target}; the
$\eps \to v$ swap on its own does not produce heavy tails.

\paragraph{v2 $\to$ v9: the distributional-realist walk.}
v9 differs from v2 on the data-transform axis only --- both are
$\eps$-prediction and FiLM-conditioned, but v9 wraps the raw features
through a copula transform with dequantization (\S\ref{sec:copula_fix}
unpacks the mechanism). The fourth and fifth rows of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation_walks} are the dominant deltas. $+$ copula
alone drops G1 by $4$ checks, pushes predictive up by
$+103\times$ replay (severely worse), adds $+27\%$ to PCA coverage,
and \emph{collapses} trade fraction by $-0.55$ (an enormous
improvement toward real's $0.048$). $+$ dequant then recovers $+4$ G1
checks, gives back $-11\times$ on predictive, and pushes kurtosis up
by $+126$ (back into a reasonable range). The combination
copula+dequant has a net effect on G1 of $0$, $-92\times$ replay on
predictive (still much worse than v2), $+13\%$ on PCA, and $-0.51$ on
trade fraction. v9 is what comes out the other side: same G1 as v2,
much worse predictive, better PCA, dramatically better trade
fraction. The takeaway: \emph{copula buys distributional realism at
the cost of predictive transferability}, and dequantization mitigates
but does not undo that trade.

\paragraph{The noclip family: the most informative third corner.}
Removing the $x_0$ output clip --- the safety bound that keeps each
sampling step from emitting extreme values --- is not a path that
involves v2/v5/v9 (none of them use noclip), but the toggle is too
informative to skip. The last row of Table~\ref{tab:ablation_walks}
shows it loses $4.8$ G1 checks, destroys PCA coverage
($-40\%$, often all the way to disjoint hulls), and slightly worsens
kurtosis --- in exchange for the largest predictive-score improvement
in the study ($-17\times$ replay). The mechanism is the same one
\S\ref{sec:res_predictive} flagged: removing the clip lets the model
emit return-sign sequences with realistic per-event variation, which
is exactly what the LSTM rewards. The cost --- losing roughly four
G1 stylized facts and all spatial overlap with real --- shows that
``best predictive transferability'' and ``best realism'' are not
served by the same toggle. The three protagonists demonstrate this
trade-off pairwise across architecture families; the noclip family
demonstrates it monotonically along a single toggle.

\subsection{Case Study: The Copula Story --- One Architecture, All Three Axes Move}
\label{sec:copula_story}
% Was its own §6 in earlier drafts; merged in here because it is the
% single most pedagogically rich instance of the §5 tension. Each of
% v8, v9, v2_remapped moves all three axes (G1, Pred, PCA) at once,
% and reading them in sequence makes the structural mechanism obvious.
% Approx 1-1.5 pages of the §5 budget.
%
% Also folds in the zero-inflation pathology that earlier drafts tried
% to introduce in §2: the empirical 89% point mass at mid_return = 0
% is the technical root cause of the copula failure, and earns its
% airtime here where it actually does load-bearing work.

Three of our twenty checkpoints belong to the same architectural
family: v8 (copula on the marginals, no dequantization), v9 (copula
\emph{with} dequantization), and v2\_remapped (no copula training,
post-hoc Spearman remap of v2's outputs to match real's marginals).
Each of the three was an attempt to solve the same problem --- the
zero-inflation in INTC mid-returns --- and each produces a different
trade-off across G1, predictive, and PCA. Reading the three of them
in sequence is the cleanest worked example of the three-axis tension
in the whole study. We start from the data property that made all
three look attractive in the first place.

\subsubsection{The Zero-Inflation Pathology (Why a Naive Copula Was Always Going to Fail)}
\label{sec:copula_zero_inflation}

INTC's mid-return distribution is not a smooth heavy-tailed density.
Across the $\sim 40$M events in our validation set, $88.85\%$ of
mid-return events are \emph{exactly} zero --- the midprice did not
change between consecutive events. Conditional on a nonzero update,
the distribution \emph{is} heavy-tailed: conditional standard
deviation $\sigma_{\neq 0} \approx 4.7\times 10^{-5}$, conditional
excess kurtosis on the order of $10^4$, conditional support extending
out to $\pm 0.030$ (i.e.\ moves of $\sim 1{,}400\sigma_{\neq 0}$). The
\emph{unconditional} excess kurtosis we report against in the G1 suite
(real $755$, our headline reference) is what you measure when the
$88.85\%$ point mass and the $11.15\%$ heavy-tailed continuous
component are mixed in a single distribution.

This mixed structure --- a discrete point mass at zero plus a
continuous heavy-tailed component --- is the property the copula
attempt is trying to fix. To see why the naive version cannot work,
trace the data flow:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The copula transform fits an empirical CDF $\hat{F}$ on the
    raw mid-return values, applies $z = \Phi^{-1}(\hat{F}(x))$ to
    every event, trains the diffusion model on the $z$-space, samples
    in $z$-space, and inverts the transform on the synthetic outputs.
    \item Because $88.85\%$ of training events are
    $x = 0$, the empirical CDF has a vertical jump at $x=0$ of size
    $0.89$. All zero-valued training events get mapped to a single
    $z$ value (specifically, the value of $\Phi^{-1}$ at the
    midpoint of the jump, which sits well below zero on the
    $z$-axis).
    \item The diffusion model trained on this $z$-space sees an input
    distribution with a massive point mass and a thin continuous
    component on either side of it. ``$\eps$-prediction MSE on
    $z$-space'' is now an objective whose optimal solution is to
    output that same point mass, because that is where most of the
    probability is.
    \item At sampling time the model emits a $z$-distribution that is
    overwhelmingly clustered at the (negative) collapsed value, with
    minimal spread. The inverse transform $x = \hat{F}^{-1}(\Phi(z))$
    then maps the cluster of $z$ values back to a cluster of $x$
    values that all sit near zero --- and the heavy tails the inverse
    CDF was supposed to recover never get sampled because the model
    never produces $z$ values in the tail regions of $\Phi$.
\end{enumerate}

The mechanism is not subtle, and we will see it directly in the v8
diagnostics in \S\ref{sec:copula_failure}. The fix --- adding a
dequantization step that smears the $0.89$-mass point evenly across a
small interval before computing $\hat{F}$ --- was already known in
the normalizing-flow literature for exactly the same reason
(integer-valued image pixels create the same kind of CDF
discontinuity); v9 implements it and recovers most of what v8 lost
on G1. \S\ref{sec:copula_promise} states the design intent of the
copula attempt; \S\ref{sec:copula_failure} shows v8's collapse
quantitatively; \S\ref{sec:copula_fix} shows v9's recovery; and
\S\ref{sec:copula_lesson} pulls out the v2\_remapped cautionary tale
that explains why even a ``good'' copula recovery still pays the
predictive-score cost we documented in \S\ref{sec:res_predictive}.

\subsubsection{The Promise: Marginals from Inverse CDF, Joints from Diffusion}
\label{sec:copula_promise}

The reason the copula approach was attractive in the first place is
Sklar's theorem
\cite{sklar1959fonctions}: any joint distribution decomposes into its
marginals and a copula that captures all the dependence structure
between them. If we transform every per-feature marginal to a
standard normal $\Phi(0, 1)$ (via the per-feature empirical CDF
followed by $\Phi^{-1}$), the diffusion model only has to learn the
\emph{copula} --- the joint dependence in $z$-space, where every
marginal is by construction a unit Gaussian. At sampling time we
invert: a synthetic $z$ goes through $\hat{F}^{-1}(\Phi(z))$ and the
heavy tails of the original distribution come back ``for free'' from
the inverse CDF. Going into v8 we expected the synthetic kurtosis to
land in the $400$--$800$ range (i.e.\ within a factor of two of real
INTC's $755$); the marginal shape would be exact by construction, and
the only thing the diffusion model would have to do is a job
($z$-space joint learning) for which it is well-suited.

\subsubsection{The Failure (v8): z-Space Collapse}
\label{sec:copula_failure}

v8 is the naive copula attempt --- raw empirical CDF on each
feature, no dequantization. The diagnostic that exposes the failure
is the per-feature distribution of $z$-values that v8 actually
\emph{emits} at sampling time, before the inverse-CDF step. If the
copula was working, the $z$-distribution should look approximately
$\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. It does not (Table~\ref{tab:v8_z_collapse}).

$z \approx -2.55$ with std $0.30$, which inverts back to a near-zero

trades). The $85.2\%$ PCA coverage is the misleading consequence:
v8's hull \emph{is} broad in PC1--PC2 space (it has to be, given the
$z$-collapse pushed the synthetic projection wide of real's center),
but the marginals inside that hull are essentially Gaussian.

\subsubsection{The Fix (v9): Dequantization}
\label{sec:copula_fix}

The fix v9 implements is dequantization, a technique developed for
exactly this kind of CDF-discontinuity problem in the
normalizing-flow literature \cite{theis2016note}. Before fitting
$\hat{F}$, we detect each per-feature point mass with mass above a
threshold (here, anything $\ge 1\%$ of the training set), and we
spread its mass uniformly across a small interval $[v - \epsilon, v]$
where $v$ is the point-mass value and $\epsilon$ is calibrated to
the gap between $v$ and the nearest neighbouring value. For
\texttt{mid\_return} this means the $88.85\%$ point mass at
$x = 0$ is replaced by uniform noise on $[-\delta, 0]$ with $\delta$
small enough not to leak into the conditional-on-nonzero
distribution. The empirical CDF $\hat{F}$ now has no vertical jumps,
$z = \Phi^{-1}(\hat{F}(x))$ is a smooth bijection on the training
support, and the diffusion model trained in $z$-space sees a roughly
Gaussian input distribution rather than a degenerate one. At
inference time we apply the same noise injection to the synthetic
$z$ samples before running the inverse transform, so the dequantized
zeros decode back to a realistic mix of small-return events.

The recovery on $z$-space marginals is large.
\texttt{mid\_return}'s $z$-mean moves from $-2.55$ (v8) to $+0.001$
(v9), the $z$-std from $0.30$ to $0.28$ (still narrower than $1.0$
but inside the regime the diffusion model can sample from with
realistic spread), the KS statistic from $0.99$ (essentially
disjoint from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$) to $0.50$. The headline
distributional metrics follow: G1 jumps from v8's $8/15$ to v9's
$12/15$ (recovering four checks --- exactly the matched-pair number
in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_walks}), excess kurtosis from $2.6$ to
$129$ (back into the $\sim 100$--$200$ range that most non-noclip
checkpoints occupy), and trade fraction from $0.009$ to $0.049$
(the closest match to real's $0.048$ in the entire study). PCA
coverage drops from $85.2\%$ to $71.4\%$, but this is the right
direction --- v8's wide hull was wide \emph{because} the marginals
collapsed and pushed the projection off-center; v9's narrower hull
sits more inside real's region.

The one axis that does \emph{not} recover is predictive
transferability. v8 had Pred $= 117\times$ replay; v9 has Pred
$= 106\times$ --- essentially the same. Dequantization fixed the
copula's marginal-collapse pathology without doing anything for the
predictive cost. \S\ref{sec:copula_lesson} pins down why.

\subsubsection{The Deeper Lesson: Spearman $\neq$ Economic Information}
\label{sec:copula_lesson}

To isolate where v9's predictive cost comes from, we ran a control
experiment that has nothing to do with copula training. We took
v2 --- the baseline that already has Pred $= 14.2\times$ replay
(the best of our three protagonists) --- and applied a post-hoc
quantile remapping to its already-trained synthetic outputs. The
mapping is the simplest version of what copula+dequant does at
inference time: for each feature, sort the synthetic values, sort
the real validation values, and replace each synthetic rank-$k$
value with the corresponding rank-$k$ real value. Per-feature
marginals match real \emph{exactly} after this step; per-event
Spearman rank correlations between any two features are
\emph{preserved exactly} as well, because remapping each marginal is
a monotone transform and Spearman correlations are invariant under
monotone marginal transforms. The resulting checkpoint is
v2\_remapped.

If the predictive-score cost were specific to copula training (the
diffusion model learning a degenerate $z$-space, optimization
artifacts, anything model-internal), v2\_remapped should match
v2's predictive score because no training was redone. It does the
opposite. v2\_remapped's Pred jumps from v2's $14.2\times$ replay
to $\mathbf{266 \times}$ replay --- worse than v9's $106\times$, the
worst predictive score in the entire study. PCA coverage collapses
from v2's $58.6\%$ to $16.1\%$. G1 falls from $12/15$ to $9/15$.
Trade fraction \emph{does} fix from v2's $0.558$ to v2\_remapped's
$0.045$ (essentially exact match to real's $0.048$, the same
recovery v9 achieves). Marginal-distribution std comes up from
v2's $6 \times 10^{-6}$ to v2\_remapped's $10^{-4}$, much closer to
real's $2.2 \times 10^{-5}$.

The lesson the control imposes: the predictive cost we attribute to
``the copula'' is not specific to v9's diffusion training. \emph{Any}
operation that resamples per-event values to match a target marginal
distribution will pay the same cost, even when applied post-hoc to
an already-good checkpoint. The reason is the LSTM evaluation:
predictive transferability rewards getting the per-event return
\emph{value} sequence right (i.e.\ at every event index, the model
should produce a value close to what the real sequence had at
the same index given the same history); rank-preserving marginal
remapping shuffles those values --- ranks are preserved, but the
per-event values get re-bucketed onto a different marginal grid,
and the LSTM, which reads values not ranks, sees a sequence whose
predictability has been scrambled. The TRADES protocol
\cite{berti2025trades} measures something the copula-based
generators are intrinsically bad at, regardless of how clean the
copula transform is. \S\ref{sec:disc_practical} returns to the
practical implication: pick the generator whose evaluation axis
matches your downstream use case.

\section{Agent-Based Stress Testing}
\label{sec:agents}

The point of building synthetic LOB generators is that we want to use
them. The use case we built into our pipeline from the start is
\emph{counterfactual market-making} --- deploying a market-making
agent against synthetic market scenarios that the agent has not seen
during calibration, so we can ask ``how would this agent behave in a
market that looks like INTC but isn't?''. This section reports what
happened when we ran four reference market-making agents through each
of our generators, what the rankings tell us, and where the
methodology runs out of road.

\subsection{Agent Design --- The Avellaneda-Stoikov Family}
\label{sec:agent_design}

We chose the Avellaneda-Stoikov family of market-making agents
\cite{avellaneda2008high} because it is the most-studied
parametric model-MM strategy, the parameters are interpretable
(inventory aversion $\gamma$, liquidity intensity $\kappa$, time
horizon $T$), and it is small enough to extend with optional signals
without changing the optimal-control derivation. Our four agents are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A0 --- Constant spread.} Posts $\pm 1$ tick around
    the prevailing mid at every event, no inventory adjustment, no
    signal. This is our null --- it should approximately collect the
    half-spread minus inventory PnL whenever its quotes are crossed.
    \item \textbf{A1 --- Avellaneda-Stoikov baseline.} Inventory-skewed
    reservation price $r = s - q\gamma\sigma^2(T - t)$ with
    half-spread $\delta = \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\sigma^2(T - t) +
    \tfrac{1}{\gamma}\ln(1 + \tfrac{\gamma}{\kappa})$, posted on each
    side. Calibrated $\gamma$ and $\kappa$ on the training set.
    \item \textbf{A2 --- A1 with order-flow-imbalance (OFI) skew.}
    Adds a short-horizon OFI term \cite{cont2014price} to the
    reservation price; quotes are leaned in the direction the OFI
    signal predicts the next-event return.
    \item \textbf{A3 --- A1 with VPIN-gated widening.} Volume-
    synchronized probability of informed trading
    \cite{easley2012flow}; when VPIN exceeds a threshold the agent
    widens its spread to compensate for adverse-selection risk.
\end{itemize}

The fill simulator is simple: at the start of each event the agent
posts bid and ask limit orders; if the next market event crosses the
posted price (via a market trade or a quote update that puts the new
best on the wrong side of the agent's quote), the agent gets filled
at the posted price; otherwise the orders are cancelled and re-posted
at the next event. Inventory and PnL are tracked event-by-event, with
a forced position close at the end of each tape. Implementation
details and parameter calibration are in
Appendix~\ref{app:agents_impl}.

\subsection{The Setup is Practical, but ``Truth'' is Itself Biased}
\label{sec:ranking_mechanism}

We deployed all four agents against each of our generators
(20 INTC checkpoints; for the cross-asset comparison in
\S\ref{sec:cross_asset}, also against TSLA and SPY synthetic tapes)
and against a historical-replay baseline that uses the real
held-out validation tapes themselves. Per agent, per tape, we record
terminal PnL, fill count, fill rate, inventory variance, and max
drawdown.

The natural temptation is to treat the historical-replay PnL ranking
as ``truth'' --- the answer the synthetic generator should be
trying to reproduce --- and to evaluate generators by how closely
their per-agent PnL ranking matches it. We resist this temptation,
because the historical-replay setup is itself a biased measurement.

The bias is structural. Historical replay is non-responsive: the
recorded order book was generated by other market participants who
did not know about our agent. When our A2 agent posts an aggressive
quote and gets filled $10{,}728$ times per day (Table~\ref{tab:agent_truth}),
the next event in the replay --- a counterparty trade or quote update
--- happens \emph{exactly the way it did historically}, regardless
of A2's now-unbalanced inventory or the price impact A2's market
participation would have caused. A real A2 agent trading at that
intensity would push prices, attract opposing flow, and shift the
adverse-selection environment its own quotes face. The historical
replay credits A2 for the fills without charging it for the impact.
Generators evaluated in the same simulator inherit the same blind
spot: they reproduce the LOB statistics, but the agent's trades are
still invisible to the synthetic counterparties.

The implication for our evaluation is sharp: we do not have a
ground-truth ranking of these agents, only a non-impact ranking
extracted from historical replay. The synthetic generators give us
\emph{additional} non-impact rankings in different LOB regimes. The
question we can actually answer is not ``which generator gives the
right ranking'' but ``what does the spread of rankings across
generators tell us about the robustness of each agent's edge''.

The headline observation is that $\rdiff$ does not track the
distributional-quality axes we measured in \S\ref{sec:results}.

operations that perfectly preserve cross-feature rank correlations
can completely change which agent looks best, because the agents read
\emph{values}, not ranks, and small per-event value perturbations
flip A1/A2-style close calls.

\subsection{Some Stylized Facts We Can Trust}
\label{sec:agent_implications}

Even without a ground-truth ranking, the spread of orderings in
Table~\ref{tab:rho_diff_spread} contains a few robust empirical
facts.

\paragraph{A0 wins (or near-wins) almost everywhere.} Across truth
and four of the seven generators, A0 (constant spread) is the
best-PnL agent. The only generators where A0 loses are v9 and v9\_b
(the copula generators that scrambled the per-event return-magnitude
structure that A0 relied on for fee collection). The robust
takeaway: in non-impact backtests on heavy-tail-marginal data, a
non-adapting MM that simply collects the spread when crossed beats
the inventory-adjusted, signal-driven AS variants. This is a known
pattern in the market-making literature for low-toxicity environments
\cite{avellaneda2008high} and our generators reproduce it.

\paragraph{The A1 vs.\ A2 distinction is a coin-flip.} A1 (no signal)
and A2 (OFI signal) end up adjacent in the ranking in every generator
in Table~\ref{tab:rho_diff_spread}, but the order between them flips
across generators (A1 above A2 in v8\_b, v11; A2 above A1 in v8, v10,
v2\_remapped). On truth A1 is above A2 by a small margin --- the
$z$-score difference in terminal PnL is $-0.08$
(Table~\ref{tab:agent_truth}). The OFI signal that A2 uses is real but
weak; small per-event return-sequence perturbations are enough to flip
which side wins. The robust takeaway: \emph{do not pick between
OFI-aware and OFI-blind market makers based on a single backtest},
synthetic or historical.

\paragraph{A3 (VPIN-gated) does not consistently help.} A3
underperforms A1 in three of seven generators and underperforms A2 in
five. On truth A3 \emph{outperforms} both, which is interesting but
within a wide PnL spread. The VPIN-as-toxicity-detector approach is
fragile to the specific way each generator emits trade size and
sign, and our generators do not agree on whether that fragility
helps or hurts.

\paragraph{What this is not.} It is tempting to read
Table~\ref{tab:rho_diff_spread} as a validation problem (``which
generator gives the right $\rdiff$?''). The framing throughout this
section has been the opposite: there is no right $\rdiff$ because
neither historical replay nor any single generator measures the
agent's PnL under realistic market impact. The right question is
which empirical patterns survive across all our non-impact
measurements --- and the three bullet points above are the patterns
that do. \S\ref{sec:disc_practical} returns to the practical
implications for practitioners who want to use synthetic generators
for strategy backtesting rather than for distributional quality
assurance.

\section{Cross-Asset Validation: INTC, TSLA, SPY}
\label{sec:cross_asset}

\subsection{Why Cross-Asset?}
\label{sec:why_cross}

The single most-cited concern about anything we said in
\S\ref{sec:results} is that INTC is a pathological dataset.
\S\ref{sec:copula_zero_inflation} reported that $88.85\%$ of INTC
mid-return events are exactly zero --- a property of large-tick
single-name equities under modern market structure that is not
universal. If the three-axis tension we documented is an artifact of
that one dataset feature, the conclusions of \S\ref{sec:results}
should not generalize. To test this, we re-ran the v2 and v5
training and evaluation pipelines on two qualitatively different
tickers: TSLA (small-tick single-name; expected smaller zero-return
fraction, more continuous returns) and SPY (the S\&P 500
ETF; expected deep liquidity, tight spreads, index-arbitrage-driven
price discovery). Each combination is a fresh end-to-end pipeline:
data prep, model training, sampling, B/C/D validation, TRADES
metrics, and predictive score on per-ticker train/val/holdout
splits.

We restricted the cross-asset comparison to v2 and v5 because they
are the two architectural extremes from \S\ref{sec:three_protagonists}
that bracket our three-axis trade-off ($\eps$+FiLM, the predictive
winner; $v$+AdaLN, the heavy-tail winner). v9 (copula+dequant) was
deferred because the copula transform's per-feature dequantization
parameters are dataset-specific and would have required a second
calibration pass per ticker --- out of scope for the cross-asset
test.

Three observations.

First, \textbf{TSLA v2 dominates TSLA v5 on every axis we
measured} --- G1 ($12$ vs.\ $10$), predictive ($4.86$ vs.\ $12.2$),
PCA ($62.0\%$ vs.\ $18.9\%$). On INTC the same architectural pair
trades off (v5 wins G1 and predictive; v2 wins PCA); on TSLA there
is no Pareto trade-off at all. The clean architectural ordering on
TSLA is itself the cleanest single piece of evidence we have that
the three-axis tension is partially INTC-specific: when the
underlying data is less zero-inflated, the heavy-tail-committing
architecture (v5) does not have a niche to dominate, and the
mode-covering baseline (v2) wins everywhere.

Second, \textbf{TSLA v2 produces the best predictive score in the
entire study} at $4.86\times$ replay (the previous best was the
INTC noclip family at $4.0\times$, which we documented as
distributionally-broken in \S\ref{sec:res_predictive}; TSLA v2 is the
first non-broken generator to come within an order of magnitude of
the market-replay baseline). The mechanical reading: TSLA's smaller
tick and lower zero-fraction give the LSTM more per-event signal to
fit; the $\eps$-prediction baseline preserves enough of that signal
to reach a regime where downstream LSTM training transfers genuinely
well.

Third, \textbf{TSLA v5's PCA coverage drops to $18.9\%$}, against
INTC v5's $46.1\%$. The $v$-prediction objective commits the model
to extreme samples; on INTC where return magnitudes are pinned to a
discrete grid, the extremes still land inside real's PC1--PC2
region. On TSLA the extremes land outside real's hull and PCA
coverage collapses. This is the same mechanism the noclip family
exhibited on INTC --- spatial overlap with real disappears when the
model's output distribution is too wide --- and v5 is the smaller
version of it on TSLA.

Trade fractions are also worth flagging: $0.183$ for TSLA v2 and
$0.340$ for TSLA v5, against INTC v2's $0.558$. TSLA's lower
zero-fraction means the per-event composition of the synthetic
output is structurally closer to real even before any
generator-specific fix --- the trade-fraction problem we devoted
\S\ref{sec:copula_story} to is much less acute on TSLA.

\subsection{SPY --- Index-Tracking ETF}
\label{sec:spy}

SPY training is in progress. Initial 6-hour training jobs on each
of v2 and v5 (the same setup that worked for INTC and TSLA) hit the
SLURM time limit without completing a single epoch --- SPY data is
roughly $10\times$ larger per day than TSLA, and a single epoch on
the full split exceeded the per-job time budget before any
checkpoint was written. We responded by patching the trainer to add
step-based checkpoint snapshots (every $10{,}000$ optimizer steps,
in addition to the per-epoch save) and submitting the training as a
chain of $4 \times 6$h jobs per architecture, each chunk resuming
from the latest snapshot of the previous chunk via Lightning's
optimizer-and-scheduler-state restore. Implementation in
\path{scripts/utils/submit_spy_chain.sh}; the chain is executing on
a parallel cluster account at the time of writing. We expect SPY
v2/v5 results to land 24--30 hours after the chain start
(\S\ref{sec:future_work} lists this as the highest-priority
follow-up and Appendix~\ref{app:cross_asset_full} will be updated
with the headline numbers once the pipeline completes). The cross-
asset comparison in this section is therefore a four-cell rather
than six-cell test for the moment.

\subsection{Cross-Asset Findings: Structural or Microstructure-Specific?}
\label{sec:cross_findings}

The four cells we have provide a partial answer to the
``structural-vs-INTC-specific'' question, with two pieces of
positive evidence and one piece against.

\paragraph{Evidence the tension is partly INTC-specific.} On TSLA,
v2 dominates v5 on all three axes; there is no architectural
trade-off to call a tension. The $\eps$-prediction baseline that
sits at the predictive-corner of the INTC trade-off becomes the
best generator everywhere on TSLA. If the three-axis tension were
structural to MSE-based diffusion training in general, we would
expect to see at least \emph{some} architectural trade-off on TSLA;
we don't.

\paragraph{Evidence the tension still partly survives.} TSLA v5's
PCA coverage collapses to $18.9\%$, much worse than INTC v5's
$46.1\%$. The per-architecture mechanism that destroyed PCA coverage
on the INTC noclip family ($v$-prediction commits to extremes that
fall outside real's hull) operates on TSLA v5 as well, just at a
smaller magnitude. So the architecture-level cost of $v$-prediction
on a smoother-marginal dataset is real --- TSLA doesn't reward
heavy-tail commitment, and v5 pays for it on PCA coverage rather
than on G1.

\paragraph{Practical implication for picking an architecture.}
A practitioner choosing between v2 and v5 for a new ticker should
not assume either of the INTC orderings carries over. On INTC the
two architectures trade off (and \S\ref{sec:disc_practical} would
say ``pick by use case''); on TSLA v2 dominates outright (and the
practitioner should just use v2). Until the SPY result lands we
cannot say which behaviour the next ticker will exhibit. The
evidence from one of two cross-asset tickers is that the trade-off
is at least somewhat INTC-specific --- but a one-of-two result is
exactly the kind of partial evidence \S\ref{sec:limitations} flags
as ``not a sufficient basis to declare the tension non-structural''.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The Three-Axis Tension as a Structural Result}
\label{sec:disc_structural}

The empirical pattern that organizes \S\ref{sec:results} --- no checkpoint
wins all three axes, and the rankings are systematically anti-correlated
across pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:pairwise_anticorrelation}) --- is not a
coincidence of which twenty configurations we happened to train. It
follows from the loss we minimized. DDPM training is per-element MSE on a
denoising target: the gradient sees each scalar of the noise (or velocity)
prediction independently and tries to drive its squared error to zero. On
heavy-tailed multivariate data this creates two mechanical pressures.
First, because MSE is dominated by squared errors at large magnitudes, an
$\eps$-objective settles into a mode-covering interior solution (the v2
behaviour: G1 pass on bulk-shape checks, kurtosis $268$ vs.\ real $755$,
$30\%$ PCA coverage). Re-weighting toward signal at low noise levels
($v$-prediction) does sharpen tails (v5 reaches kurtosis $1257$), but the
per-element nature of the criterion means the model commits to extreme
values without coordinating those extremes across features --- so v5's
PCA hull collapses to $0\%$. Second, marginals can be hit post-hoc by
rank-preserving remap (the copula construction of \S\ref{sec:copula_story},
extending Sklar's theorem~\cite{sklar1959fonctions} to a learned joint),
but the act of re-bucketing per-event values onto a target grid scrambles
the value sequence the LSTM reads as ``predictability,'' which is why v9
and v2\_remapped both pay the predictive cost we measured in
\S\ref{sec:copula_lesson}. The two pressures are not the same mechanism,
but they share the same root: per-element loss against multivariate
heavy-tailed targets. The TSLA result of \S\ref{sec:cross_findings}
qualifies how universal that root is. On a smoother-tailed ticker the
$v$-vs-$\eps$ trade-off does not produce a Pareto front --- v2 dominates
v5 on every axis. The mechanism (per-element MSE under-coordinates extremes)
is still real on TSLA (v5's PCA still collapses to $18.9\%$), but its
\emph{cost in trade-off terms} depends on how heavy the marginals are.
Read honestly, the three-axis tension is structural \emph{conditional} on
INTC-like microstructure, not structural to MSE-based diffusion training
in the abstract.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Practitioners}
\label{sec:disc_practical}

The negative content of \S\ref{sec:disc_structural} is also its positive
content: there is no one-size-fits-all generator, but the choice is
disciplined by which axis the downstream task actually cares about.

\paragraph{Distributional realism (e.g.\ regulatory stress tests, scenario
generation for a target moment).} v2 is the safe pick on INTC-like
microstructure --- best G1 of the twenty, $79\%$ pass, paid for with a
middling predictive score. If the downstream specifically rewards heavy
tails (extreme-loss VaR, fat-tail scenario sampling), v5's higher
kurtosis is the better match, but PCA coverage should be checked before
deployment because v5 is exactly the family that loses it.

\paragraph{Temporal transferability (e.g.\ training a downstream sequence
model on synthetic data).} The noclip $\eps$-prediction family
(v3\_e9\_noclip is the single best of twenty, $4\times$ replay) is the
right pick. The honest disclosure is that those generators fail G1 hard
and cover zero of real's PCA hull --- they are good \emph{for that one
purpose only}, and we would not recommend using them for any quality
claim other than predictive transferability.

\paragraph{Multivariate scenario coverage (e.g.\ diverse-scenario
backtesting that needs to span the joint distribution).} v9 (copula) is
the only protagonist that reaches the high-coverage region we measured.
It pays for that with a $106\times$ predictive score, so any backtest
that reads per-event return magnitude beyond rank should not run on a v9
tape.

\paragraph{Agent / strategy backtesting (the \S\ref{sec:agents} use case).}
The honest answer here is the most cautious one: no single generator
provides a trustworthy ranking, because the historical-replay reference
itself is biased (it does not respond to the agent's trades) and each
generator scrambles a different piece of the per-event structure. The
workflow we found defensible in \S\ref{sec:agent_implications} is to run
the same agent set across several generators and report only the patterns
that survive the spread (A0 dominance under low-toxicity regimes
\cite{avellaneda2008high}, A1$\approx$A2 within noise, A3 fragility to
trade-sign emission). Single-generator strategy claims are not supported
by what we measured.

\paragraph{Cross-asset transfer.} \S\ref{sec:cross_findings} showed that
the INTC ordering does not carry over to TSLA --- v2 dominates outright
rather than trading off against v5. A practitioner moving to a new ticker
should re-evaluate v2 and v5 (and v9 if the ticker has INTC-like
zero-inflated returns) on the new data before committing to one of the
above use-case picks; do not assume the INTC-conditional architecture
ranking is the right one.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Five caveats bound how far the structural reading of \S\ref{sec:disc_structural}
can be pushed. (i) \emph{Backbone is fixed.} All twenty checkpoints share
the TRADES transformer~\cite{berti2025trades}; we did not test a U-Net or
state-space backbone, so the per-element-MSE mechanism we attribute the
tension to has only been observed inside one architectural family.
(ii) \emph{INTC-dominance.} The zero-inflated marginal that drives the
\S\ref{sec:copula_story} pathology is INTC-specific microstructure; TSLA
already qualifies the structural claim and SPY was still chunked-training
at submission time, so the cross-asset evidence base is two and a half
tickers, not three. (iii) \emph{Twenty holdout days is thin for agent
rankings.} The $\rdiff$ spread of \S\ref{sec:rdiff_decoupled} has wide
per-agent confidence intervals; reading any one cell as a stable signal
would over-reach. (iv) \emph{Predictive score is LSTM-conditional.} The
score is the MAE of a fixed two-layer LSTM trained on synthetic and
tested on real; a different downstream sequence model could re-rank the
checkpoints. (v) \emph{Backtests are non-responsive.} Every agent number
in \S\ref{sec:agents} --- on every generator, including ``truth'' ---
assumes our trades do not move the market. Real impact would compress
the spreads we observed and could reorder cells inside the table.
(vi) \emph{No intraday structure in synth output.} Our generators are
trained on regime-conditioned event windows that are not anchored to
time-of-day, so the synthetic output does not reproduce the intraday
U-shape that real INTC exhibits in volume and queue size
(Figure~\ref{fig:intraday_lob_volume}, also flagged in panel F of
Figure~\ref{fig:real_stylized_facts}). Any downstream task that
depends on time-of-day microstructure (open/close auctions, lunchtime
volume troughs, end-of-day queue build-up) cannot use these tapes
directly.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

Four directions follow naturally from the limitations above. (i)
\emph{Autoregressive (event-by-event) diffusion} would let each event's
denoising condition on the realized previous event rather than the
noised joint; this might ease the per-element MSE pressure that
under-coordinates extremes across features. (ii) \emph{A mixture-of-experts
denoising head} ($\eps$-prediction for bulk events, $v$-prediction for
tail events, with a learned router) would let one model carry both modes
without forcing a single denoising target to optimize both shapes.
(iii) \emph{Direct distributional losses} (MMD, Wasserstein) added to
the MSE objective would reward marginal and joint shape directly rather
than as a side effect of squared error --- a candidate fix to the
specific failure mode we identified in \S\ref{sec:disc_structural}.
(iv) \emph{Broader evaluation:} more agents (momentum, market-taking,
mean-reversion in addition to the MM family of \S\ref{sec:agent_design}),
longer holdout windows than twenty days, and an impact-aware backtester
so $\rdiff$ stops being a non-responsive number. The SPY chained-training
job will close the cross-asset triangle of \S\ref{sec:cross_asset} and
will resolve the structural-vs-microstructure question of
\S\ref{sec:disc_structural} one way or the other.
